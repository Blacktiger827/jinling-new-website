# 8K Mirror Finish Stainless Steel

**Look twice. That is the point.**

A good mirror stops being a surface and becomes whatever is in front of it — a lobby, a ceiling, a face, a piece of light. Getting there is slower and fussier than most buyers expect, and that is where most mirror orders go sideways.

We have been polishing No.8 on our own lines since the late 1990s. Here is what we actually do differently, and how to write a purchase order that gets you the mirror you pictured.

## Why Our Mirror Looks Different

**We run the polishing line at 2 metres a minute, not as a race to finish the coil.**

Speed is cheaper. Speed is also why so many "8K" samples look fine on the sample table and haze up the moment they hit raking light in a lobby. We slow down on purpose. Twenty-four oscillating heads, each moving in a small circle rather than a straight line, wipe the surface from more than one direction. That is what reduces directional grit marks and scan-line risk in the reflection.

It takes longer. The mirror is clearer.

That slower line changes the parts of the order buyers actually see later:

- The reflection is less directional, because the polishing action is not dragging one straight grain through the sheet.
- The low-sulfur base steel keeps sulfide inclusions from becoming tiny pinholes after the surface is opened up by polishing.
- Air filtration and cooling stop grit and heat from turning into hidden defects before film goes on.
- Raking-light release catches haze, waviness, and polish marks under the kind of light that will expose them after installation.

## Two Things We Will Do That Most Won't

**Mirror on 430.** Most mirror suppliers only polish 300-series. We developed low-sulfur 430 sourcing and a matched film spec for indoor decorative panels, so controlled interior projects can hold the mirror brief without paying for corrosion reserve they will not use. The honest trade is environment: 430 mirror stays indoors.

**12 mm hot-rolled plate, mirror-polished.** The industry ceiling for mirror is usually 6 mm. We run a dedicated heavy-plate line for architectural and industrial jobs where the bent mirror panel has to carry load and still carry the eye.

**5-foot (1524 mm) wide coil.** The North American architectural standard. Fewer seams in a facade. We run it coil-to-coil on a dedicated wide line, not pieced from narrower stock.

## When Mirror Is the Right Call

Use 8K when **the reflection itself is part of the design brief**:

- Landmark lobby walls, ceilings, and feature panels
- Elevator ceilings and high-visibility accents
- Appliance fronts and indoor decorative trim
- Retail, hospitality, and hotel fit-outs
- Signage and display work that needs a clean reflective face

Pick something else when the real need is scratch tolerance, low-maintenance texture, or a finish that forgives installation handling. [No.4 brushed](/surfaces/stainless-steel-no4-brushed-finish) usually carries practical service better.

If the project wants a quieter long grain, use [hairline](/surfaces/stainless-steel-hairline-finish). If the job needs a brighter mill base rather than true mirror, [BA](/surfaces/stainless-steel-ba-finish) is often the cleaner choice.

## Pick the Substrate Before You Argue About Polish

The finish can look identical on several grades. The life in service cannot. For most mirror work, the substrate decision is simple enough to state plainly.

- **430 mirror is the indoor value route.** It fits dry decorative panels, appliance fronts, and selected elevator interiors when the environment is controlled and the savings are real.
- **304 / 304L is the mainstream interior route.** Use it for hospitality, retail, elevator, and standard architectural interiors where the installed surface needs a safer corrosion margin.
- **316L is the environment upgrade.** Use it for coastal air, humid lobbies, pool-adjacent zones, or cleaning routines where chlorides are part of the service life.
- **201 is narrow.** It only belongs in budget indoor decorative work when inclusion control and polish quality can be checked before release.

## Set the Release Standard on the PO

The mirror conversation goes wrong when a purchase order says only *mirror stainless*. That is not a release standard. Use one of three lanes instead.

- **Standard No.8** covers most interior mirror panels. Name the grade, visual review condition, and film type. If a number is required, tie Ra or gloss to an agreed measuring method.
- **Super 8K** belongs on landmark interiors and high-clarity reflection work. Add measured Ra or GU, lot consistency, and handling language so the standard survives handover.
- **Mirror plus AFP** is for touch-exposed panels. Confirm coating compatibility, final sample, and cleaning expectation before production, because the top coat changes the release conversation.

## The Film Is Part of the Finish

On mirror, protection is not an afterthought. It is part of what you are buying.

- **Choose the film before production.** Laser-cutting film, AFP-compatible low-tack film, and general PE storage film are not interchangeable. Swapping after shipping means rework.
- **Lock downstream work early.** What happens after the sheet leaves us — laser cutting, bending, AFP coating, direct install — pulls different film choices.
- **Treat packaging as part of acceptance.** Sliding contact in transit will ruin a clean polish even when the polishing line was perfect. Our export crates are built for that.
- **For high-touch panels**, decide early whether the face stays bare mirror or moves to [AFP](/surfaces/stainless-steel-afp-finish) after polishing.

## What to Write on the Order

- Name the substrate: `430 mirror`, `304 mirror`, and `316L mirror` are different materials, even when day-one appearance is similar.
- State the finish target: `No.8`, `Ra ≤ 0.05 µm`, or `Ra < 0.03 µm` for Super 8K.
- Call the film: laser, AFP-compatible, or standard PE — do not leave it to us to guess.
- Note whether the face stays bare mirror or moves to [AFP](/surfaces/stainless-steel-afp-finish).
- For wide panels, phased projects, and elevator packages, make lot consistency and inspection condition part of release.

## How We Catch What the Instruments Miss

Every sheet leaves the polishing line past a raking-light inspection booth, where an operator walks each panel under a focused light and looks for haze, pinholes, polish lines, and film marks. We also run laser-cutting tests on representative samples before mass production, especially on harder-to-polish grades like 201. If the sheet cannot pass the agreed viewing condition, it is held back instead of being packed as finished mirror.
