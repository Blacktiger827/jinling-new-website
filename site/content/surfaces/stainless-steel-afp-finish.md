# AFP Anti-Fingerprint Finish Stainless Steel Sheet & Coil

**It is the panel that still looks clean at six in the evening.**

Everyone has seen the opposite — an elevator wall covered in smudges by lunchtime, a stainless appliance door that takes a polishing cloth after every touch, a hotel reception counter that never quite stops looking fingerprinted. AFP exists because that is avoidable, and because in a finished building, daily maintenance is part of how the material gets judged.

The coating is clear and thin. What actually matters is who formulates it, how it is applied, and whether it is matched to the exact base finish you chose.

## What AFP Actually Does

Think of it as a screen protector for stainless steel, but specified like a finish, not like an accessory.

When No.4 or hairline steel is polished, the surface has microscopic peaks and valleys. Below a roughness of about Ra 0.35 µm, oil and grease from fingers settle into those valleys and stay there — which is why brushed stainless shows fingerprints so clearly. AFP is a nano-resin coating 10 to 20 microns thick. It fills the valleys, leaves the visible surface texture untouched, and gives the whole panel a hydrophobic, oil-repellent skin.

The point is not that fingerprints disappear forever. The point is that touch marks become easier to wipe, less visible under ordinary light, and less likely to define how the panel looks after a day of use.

AFP earns its place when it does four quiet jobs well:

- It reduces how much skin oil sits inside the brushed or hairline valleys.
- It gives water, oil, and common soiling a surface that wipes clean with less drama.
- It slows atmospheric staining where humidity, cleaning, or hand contact keep attacking the face.
- It keeps the base finish readable, so No.4 still looks like No.4, hairline still has its long grain, and mirror still reflects.

## Why Our AFP Looks Different

**We formulate it here.** Most domestic suppliers buy AFP resin off the shelf. We run our own 380-metre coating line at 60 tonnes per day, and our engineering team adjusts the formula — resin base, nano-additive ratio, gloss target — against each client's approved sample. That is how colour lands within ΔE ≤ 2 across lots, instead of drifting from one container to the next.

**We iterate before mass.** The first sample is rarely the final formula. We keep adjusting resin, nano-additive ratio, gloss, and cure until the approved panel feels like the project brief, not just like a passing lab result. That record then becomes the release reference for every subsequent lot.

**We keep the coating line clean.** AFP is unforgiving of dust: airborne particles during cure become visible defects. Coating is done in a controlled section with purified air, and every sheet is cooled and inspected before film application. The film itself is matched to AFP — standard PE adhesive can pull the coating on peel.

## When AFP Earns Its Cost

AFP earns its cost where the same panel is touched, wiped, and cleaned every single day:

- elevator wall panels and ceilings in busy buildings
- appliance fronts — refrigerators, ovens, range hoods, dishwashers
- hotel, retail, and reception counters
- public circulation areas, lift lobbies, decorative cladding in commercial space
- outdoor decorative panels where slowing oxidation matters
- medical and lab equipment where cleaners are aggressive and frequent

It is far less useful on surfaces rarely touched, hidden after fabrication, or already cleaned by industrial washdown routines. On those jobs, an uncoated [No.4](/surfaces/stainless-steel-no4-brushed-finish) or [hairline](/surfaces/stainless-steel-hairline-finish) is usually enough.

## AFP Sits on Top of a Base Finish, Not Instead of One

AFP does not replace the base finish choice. It changes how that finish lives in service. Pick the base first.

The base finish usually decides the visual job:

- **No.4 plus AFP** is the practical route for appliance fronts, kitchen equipment, public counters, and satin panels that get touched all day.
- **Hairline plus AFP** keeps the long architectural grain while making elevator cabs, lobby cladding, and decorative walls easier to maintain.
- **No.8 mirror plus AFP** belongs where reflection still matters, but hand contact would make bare mirror too demanding.
- **2B plus AFP** is more utilitarian: appliance backs, selected utility panels, and working surfaces where cleaning is more important than decoration.

## Match Grade to the Environment

The coating does not replace alloy choice.

Choose the grade by exposure, then use AFP to manage the surface.

- **304** is the default for most indoor decorative and appliance AFP work.
- **316L** is the safer route when humidity, chlorides, pool air, or aggressive cleaning are part of the service condition.
- **430** can be a strong value choice for dry indoor panels and appliance skins, but AFP does not turn it into an outdoor alloy.
- **201** should stay in low-expectation indoor decorative work where the buyer understands the service limit.

## Lock AFP Like a Performance Spec

AFP should be written with the same discipline as the base stainless specification.

Lock the parts that decide whether the coating behaves the same on the second container as it did on the approved sample:

- Dry-film thickness, typically 10 to 20 microns, so performance and appearance do not drift apart.
- Approved sample for colour, gloss, and hand feel, because AFP is judged visually as much as technically.
- Base finish and grade, since the coating only performs as well as the surface underneath.
- Adhesion and pencil-hardness expectation when incoming QC needs a checkable reference.
- Protective film compatibility, because standard PE film is not automatically correct for AFP.

## What to Write on the Order

- Base finish first, AFP second.
- Grade by environment, not by coating.
- Approved visual sample for gloss and colour.
- Coating thickness and release checks.
- Protective film for downstream fabrication and delivery.
- Whether the panel is a touch surface or purely decorative — AFP pricing follows the answer.
