# Surface Finishing Capability

Surface finishing is a release point where appearance, cleanability, and handling risk become part of the order.

The useful question is not whether the finish looks good in isolation. It is whether the substrate, route, film, and inspection method all match the job that comes next.

<p class="article-note"><em>Finish control is judged on the face under real light. The substrate, film, and acceptance rule need to be settled before release.</em></p>

## When Finish Becomes a Release Decision

Surface finishing should be treated as a release route, not decoration after the order. The finish only works when substrate, sample, film, inspection condition, and packing all match the job that comes next.

| Open decision | Finish route | Release check |
|---|---|---|
| Reflection or high-visibility effect? | mirror / No.8 finish | control feedstock, Ra or gloss, film, and handling |
| Touch-heavy panels? | AFP coating | match base finish, coating, sample, and film chemistry |
| Practical visible grain? | No.4 / hairline / BA finish | lock grain direction, lot consistency, and viewing rule |

## What Must Be Controlled

- **Mirror / No.8:** control feedstock quality, Ra or gloss target, film, and face handling before release. Haze, pinholes, and scratches become visible quickly.
- **AFP stack:** set base finish, coating route, film type, and sample match as one stack. The risk is coating mismatch, residue, or tack conflict.
- **No.4 / Hairline / BA:** lock grain direction, face cleanliness, lot logic, and viewing rule before shipment. The common failures are grain drift, lot mismatch, and repair marks.

## Where Orders Go Wrong

Most finish problems come from one of three misses.

The first is asking for appearance without naming the substrate. A mirror on the wrong alloy still behaves like the wrong alloy.

The second is leaving film unspecified. Laser cutting, bending, storage, and installation do not want the same film.

The third is treating a sample as a suggestion instead of the acceptance rule. On visible panels, that is how disputes start.

## Order-Time Controls

1. Name the substrate and finish together.
2. State whether the finish is final-use or a base for later processing.
3. Define the film type and the downstream step it must survive.
4. Write the viewing or sample rule before shipment.

## Finish Families

- Mirror / No.8 for reflective, high-visibility surfaces where haze and pinholes are the main risk.
- AFP for touch-heavy panels where the stack needs cleaning control, not shine alone.
- No.4 / Hairline for working decorative surfaces where grain direction and repairability matter more than specular reflection.
- BA / 2B for bright or forming-base routes when the finish is part of a later process.

## Boundary

Finish control does not replace alloy choice. If the service is chloride-heavy, coastal, or wet-cleaned, the substrate still needs to carry the corrosion load.

A good finish order should make clear what must be locked before release, not how impressive the line sounds. When the substrate, film, and acceptance rule are explicit, the finish behaves like a controlled process instead of a cosmetic layer.
