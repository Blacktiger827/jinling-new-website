# Stainless Steel for Water Treatment & Desalination

**Water-treatment stainless should be chosen by chloride at the wetted surface, not by the headline name of the plant. The concentrate side, the gasket crevice, and the warm zone are usually where the real decision gets made.**

The material route needs to follow the water chemistry. Vessel fabrication, NSF certification of finished assemblies, and plant commissioning remain with the EPC or OEM.

## Follow the Water as It Concentrates

Water-treatment material choice changes as chloride concentrates through the system.

**Low-chloride or potable water** can start from 316L, then disinfectant, temperature, and cleaning exposure decide whether that baseline is still safe.

**Seawater-side service** usually brings 2205 into view, especially where intake structures, pretreatment, or warm chloride exposure increase crevice risk.

**RO concentrate, brine, and hot crevices** need their own review. The local chloride level at the gasket or crevice can be more important than the bulk water name.

## Follow the Chloride Escalation Map

| Water service | Typical chloride condition | Common grade path |
|---|---|---|
| Potable water, permeate, and low-chloride municipal service | low chloride | 316L |
| Brackish or marginal municipal service | moderate chloride and temperature-dependent risk | 316L or 2205 |
| Seawater pretreatment and intake-adjacent service | seawater chloride band | 2205 often enters review |
| RO pressure housings, warm seawater, and concentrate service | higher local chloride and crevice risk | 2507 or 254 SMO review |
| Brine and concentrate headers | highest chloride band in the train | 2507 specialist review |

The useful logic is the escalation path from freshwater to seawater to concentrate, not a single long list of grades.

## Move From 316L to 2205 to 2507 as the Train Concentrates

316L still makes sense for freshwater, potable water, and many lower-chloride municipal routes. 2205 becomes the practical step-up when the system reaches seawater or stronger chloride exposure. 2507 and related higher-alloy routes belong where the train concentrates chlorides further or the crevice margin becomes too small.

The upgrade is often driven by local concentration, temperature, and gasket geometry together, not by the bulk stream label alone.

## Keep Municipal, Seawater, and Thermal Service Separate

| Plant service | First judgment | Common material logic |
|---|---|---|
| Municipal and potable water | source-water chloride and disinfectant severity | 316L baseline, 2205 where chloride or oxidant burden rises |
| RO desalination | position in the train and concentrate build-up | 2205 for seawater-side structures, 2507 for harsher pressure and brine service |
| MED / MSF thermal desalination | temperature plus chloride | a separate thermal case where higher-alloy choices appear sooner |

One desalination page still needs three different first judgments, depending on the process family.

- Gasketed joints and flange faces usually fail before open sheet does 鈥?see [crevice corrosion in stainless steel](/knowledge-base/crevice-corrosion-stainless-steel).
- Warm chloride service changes the grade ladder faster than many municipal buyers expect.
- Weld zones still need the right grade and delivery condition.
- A higher-alloy upgrade at the wrong joint can be more useful than upgrading the entire system blindly.

## What to Put on the Order

- Chloride range at the actual wetted surface if known.
- Operating temperature and process location in the train.
- Grade and product form.
- Any PREN requirement, especially on duplex routes.
- NDT, pickling, or delivery-state requirements.
- Whether the service is municipal, seawater, RO concentrate, or thermal desalination.
