# Stainless Steel for Chemical & Petrochemical

**A 316L vessel built for dilute nitric service ends up on a chloride-bearing line and starts pitting at every flange crevice within months. The acid name on the P&ID was right; the contaminant story underneath it was missing.** Chemical-service stainless has to be matched to the actual stream 鈥?medium, contaminants, temperature, pressure basis, and crevice risk 鈥?not to a generic "chemical-grade" line item.

Material selection should be written clearly before fabrication starts. Pressure-vessel design, code stamping, and process qualification remain with the fabricator and owner's engineer.

## Read the Stream Before the Equipment Name

Chemical service should be read from the actual stream before the product form is chosen.

**Stable, low-contaminant service** can begin with 304L or 316L screening, but only after temperature, chloride carryover, and cleaning chemistry are known.

**Chloride plus pressure** is where 2205 often enters the conversation. PREN, weld control, wall, and proof language need to travel together.

**Mixed acid or aggressive chemistry** should not be forced into ordinary stainless. When hydrochloric, hydrofluoric, or tougher sulfuric bands appear, the honest answer may be a higher alloy or lined system.

## Name Medium, Contaminants, and Temperature Together

The primary medium rarely tells the whole story. Chlorides, oxygen level, sulfur species, cleaning chemistry, and temperature swings often change the grade choice faster than the headline acid name does.

That is why the first commercial split is not plate versus pipe. It is whether the service is mild and stable, chloride-bearing and pressure-loaded, or aggressive enough that stainless may stop being the right class of alloy.

## Use a Grade Ladder Instead of a Single "Chemical Grade"

| Service band | Usual material logic | Why it fits |
|---|---|---|
| Non-chloride oxidizing or lighter chemical service | 304L or 316L | entry choice when contaminants stay controlled |
| Mild organic acids, dilute mineral acids, many process vessels | 316L | practical baseline for a large share of plant service |
| Chloride-bearing process streams and higher-pressure service | 2205 duplex | stronger and more resistant once 316L runs out of margin |
| Aggressive mixed-acid or tougher sulfuric / phosphoric bands | 904L or higher alloy review | used when chemistry outgrows 316L and duplex logic |
| Hydrochloric or hydrofluoric acid duty | not a stainless answer | move to nickel alloy or lined-system logic |

The ladder matters because 316L is often the right first answer, but not the safest last answer.

## Know Where 316L Ends and 2205 or 904L Begin

Move beyond 316L when one of these conditions becomes real:

- chloride content and temperature rise together
- the piping or vessel needs higher strength as well as better corrosion margin
- the service includes mixed acids that punish ordinary austenitic routes
- the risk sits at flanges, welds, and crevices rather than on open-surface coupon data

2205 is the common next step when chloride and strength rise together. 904L and higher-alloy routes enter when the chemistry itself becomes the governing problem.

## Pressure Service Still Needs a Code Basis

| Equipment type | Common form | Typical standards |
|---|---|---|
| Reactor and vessel shells | sheet and plate | ASME SA-240 |
| Process piping | industrial pipe | ASTM A312 / ASTM A790 |
| Heat-exchanger and tube service | tube and pipe | ASTM A312 / ASTM A790 |
| Pump, flange, and machined parts | bar stock | ASTM A276 / related machining standard |

In chemical work, the alloy choice and the code route should be written together.

## Write Against the Failure Modes That Actually Repeat

- [Crevice attack](/knowledge-base/crevice-corrosion-stainless-steel) at flanges and gasketed joints.
- Weld-zone corrosion where the wrong grade or wrong post-treatment is assumed to be acceptable.
- Iron contamination from fabrication and handling, especially when pickling or passivation expectations are vague.
- Over-trust in nominal chemistry without checking the actual service contaminants by heat.

These are often the failures that turn a reasonable alloy into a bad project result.

## What to Put on the PO

- Medium plus key contaminants.
- Operating temperature and any upset range.
- Grade and product form.
- Pressure-code route and material standard.
- Any PREN, hardness, or NDT requirement.
- Pickling / passivation expectations where the fabricator needs a defined delivery state.
