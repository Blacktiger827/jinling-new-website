# What Made Us Stop an AFP Lot That Looked Acceptable?

The lot could have shipped.

The AFP coating passed the usual checks. The gloss meter was inside the agreed band. The colour reading sat within the buyer's tolerance. Under straight inspection light, the sheet looked close to the approved panel.

Then one inspector tilted the panel.

At about thirty degrees, under a different light, the surface looked a little too matte. It was not dramatic. It was the kind of difference a busy line can talk itself into accepting. On a refrigerator door, next to another panel from the same appliance set, it would have been obvious.

So we stopped the lot.

## Why the Standard Check Was Not Enough

AFP is a clear coating over a surface that already has texture. On No.4 brushed stainless, the grain below the coating still talks to the light. A coating can measure correctly from the front and still look different when the viewing angle changes.

That is why appliance and interior-panel AFP cannot be released by instrument readings alone. The physical reference sample matters. So do trained eyes, repeated lighting, and the willingness to say no when the lot is almost right.

In this case, the client had sent us a reference panel. It was the real target, not a suggestion. The first formula matched it on paper. It did not match it well enough in the room.

## What We Changed

The problem was not coating thickness. Adding more resin would have changed other things and made the surface feel wrong. The answer was in the formula.

Our engineering team ran three small trials:

| Trial | What changed | What we saw |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | nano-additive ratio adjusted | closer at angle, weaker straight-on gloss |
| 2 | binder and levelling balance changed | front view improved, angle still drifted |
| 3 | binder family changed and additive ratio restored | front, 30 degree, and 60 degree views matched |

The third trial became the production route. Only after the retest passed under multiple lights and angles did the lot move back toward shipment.

## What the Pull Cost

Stopping the lot cost resin, line time, labour, and schedule. The delay was uncomfortable. Nobody enjoys telling a long-running client that a shipment needs a few more days because we rejected our own work.

But the alternative was worse: a branded appliance maker receiving fifteen tonnes of panels that looked acceptable in a warehouse and wrong in a showroom. That is the kind of claim that damages a relationship because everyone can see the problem and nobody can fix it cheaply.

The cheaper decision was to stop early.

## What Changed Afterward

The most important result was not the saved shipment. It was the new reference file.

The final formula, lighting method, angle check, and approved sample were recorded as that client's control route. Future AFP lots do not start from a generic recipe. They start from the reference that survived this problem.

That is what buyers should ask from an AFP supplier:

- Is there a physical approved sample for my order?
- Is every lot compared against that sample?
- Are angle and light checks part of release?
- What happens if the meter passes but the panel still looks wrong?

If the answer is "we use standard AFP", that may be fine for simple functional work. For appliance panels, hospitality interiors, and adjacent visible panels, standard AFP is often not enough.
