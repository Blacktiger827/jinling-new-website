# When Is a Stainless Steel Surface Defect Acceptable?

A surface defect is acceptable only when the defect is outside the part's real duty.

That sounds obvious until a container arrives. One side sees a few light marks on a commercial finish. The other side sees panels that will sit in a hotel lobby, a food-contact tank, or a mirror door where one scratch ruins the part. ASTM A480 and EN 10088-2 name surface finishes, but they rarely settle those arguments by themselves.

For surface-sensitive orders, the buyer has to define the inspection face, lighting, viewing distance, and reject limit before the material ships. Otherwise the argument usually turns into "commercially acceptable" against "not acceptable for our use."

## Start With the Use, Not the Defect Name

The same mark can be harmless on one order and a reject on another.

| Surface duty | What deserves stricter rejection | What may be record-only |
|---|---|---|
| Decorative face, elevator, architecture | visible scratches, color mismatch, haze, roller marks | hidden-face marks, edge blemishes after trimming |
| Food or pharma contact | pits, crevices, rust stain, rough weld-adjacent surface | removable handling marks outside product contact |
| Forming or bending stock | edge cracks, deep scratches across bend line | light in-grain scratches away from forming zone |
| Structural or industrial parts | defects reducing thickness, cracks, laminations | cosmetic scuffs with no service effect |

That is the first human judgement in inspection: where will this surface live?

## The Defects That Usually Matter

Most stainless surface claims fall into a small group:

- **Scratches:** cross-grain scratches show more and are harder to blend than scratches along the rolling or brushing direction.
- **Pits or pinholes:** small depressions can trap residue, start corrosion, or ruin mirror reflection.
- **Roller marks:** repeated marks at a regular interval point back to a roll or line condition, not random handling.
- **Edge cracks:** dangerous when the part will be bent, flanged, or welded.
- **Rust staining:** often iron contamination rather than base-metal corrosion, but it still signals a handling or packing problem.
- **Color or gloss mismatch:** critical when panels sit side by side.

The inspection report should name the defect plainly. "Bad surface" is hard to defend. "Three cross-grain scratches on visible face, longest 42 mm, visible at 1 m under raking light" is a claim record.

## What the PO Should Say

For a surface-sensitive order, five lines are usually enough:

> Inspection face: visible / product-contact face only.
> Viewing condition: 1 m distance, 500 lux light, raking angle for polished or mirror surfaces.
> Reject limits: no pits over 0.1 mm; no scratches deeper than 0.05 mm on functional face; no repeated roller marks in visual field.
> Measurement method: depth gauge, profilometer, or agreed comparator sample.
> Sampling: AQL or fixed percentage, with photos retained before packing.

That wording is stronger than "good surface." It gives both sides the same ruler.

For mirror-specific inspection language, use [how to accept or reject a mirror finish sheet](/knowledge-base/mirror-finish-acceptance-haze-gloss-pinhole).

## How to Inspect Without Creating a New Argument

Inspect before cutting, polishing, welding, or mixing the lot into stock. Once the material has been processed, the supplier can fairly ask whether the defect arrived with the shipment or happened in your shop.

Use this order:

1. Match heat number and bundle ID to the MTC and packing list.
2. Photograph the package before opening if there is any crush, water, or film damage.
3. Inspect the agreed face under the agreed light.
4. Mark defects with removable labels, not permanent pen.
5. Photograph each defect with a ruler and a wider shot showing location.
6. Record whether the defect is reject, concession, or record-only.

Surface disputes are won or lost in the first photos. A close-up without scale is almost useless. A photo with scale, angle, piece ID, and packing condition tells the story.

## When to Hold the Lot

Hold the material when:

- the defect sits on a visible or product-contact face
- the defect repeats across many sheets or at fixed intervals
- rust or pitting appears before fabrication
- the defect may reduce thickness or become a crack during forming
- the PO named a visual sample and the lot does not match it
- the supplier and buyer disagree on whether the mark is removable

Holding does not mean the material is automatically rejected. It means the lot should not be used until the evidence, concession, repair, or replacement path is clear.

## Surface-Acceptance Shortcut

Finish names do not replace acceptance criteria. A 2B sheet, No.4 panel, BA coil, or 8K mirror sheet still needs a use case and a viewing rule. Write the rule before shipment. Inspect before processing. Photograph before moving the material.
