# Why Can't Hot-Rolled Plate Hold Strip Tolerance?

A drawing that expects narrow-strip tolerance from wide hot-rolled plate is already in trouble before the mill quotes it.

Both products are stainless steel. They do not come from the same manufacturing world. Hot-rolled plate is controlled while the metal is hot, wide, heavy, and still contracting. Precision strip is cold-rolled in narrow widths through a much more stable process. Comparing their tolerance numbers without the process behind them creates bad orders and unfair claims.

## The Difference Is Physical, Not Carelessness

Hot-rolled plate carries more tolerance because three things are happening at once.

The plate leaves the finishing stand hot and then shrinks as it cools. The rolls and mill housing deflect under heavy load. Across a wide plate, the centre and edges do not cool or deform exactly the same way.

Cold-rolled strip avoids most of that. It is rolled at room temperature, in narrower widths, with better gauge feedback and less thermal movement. Precision strip can be held to very small bands because the process is built for that kind of control.

| Product form | What usually controls tolerance | Practical buying lesson |
|---|---|---|
| Wide hot-rolled plate | heat, roll deflection, width profile, flatness | use minimum guaranteed thickness in calculations |
| Cold-rolled sheet | rolling gauge control and residual stress | standard tolerance often works for normal fabrication |
| Narrow precision strip | controlled width, cold reduction, tighter feedback | specify strip standard and class, not sheet language |

The mill cannot make a 2,000 mm hot-rolled plate behave like 80 mm precision strip just because the drawing wants it.

## Why Wider Plate Gets Looser

Width makes every problem louder. A wider plate spans more of the roll crown. Its edges cool differently from the centre. It is more likely to carry shape issues such as edge wave, cross bow, or centre buckle. Even when the thickness is within standard, those shape effects make measurement and downstream fitting more difficult.

That does not mean hot-rolled plate is a poor product. It means it is the wrong product if the buyer really needs strip-level precision.

## Use Minimum Thickness in Design

For structural and pressure work, the number that matters is not nominal thickness. It is the lowest thickness the standard permits or the lowest measured thickness the supplier commits to.

If a 10 mm plate can legally arrive at 9.7 mm, then the design should use 9.7 mm unless the PO says otherwise. If the assembly cannot tolerate extra weight or over-thickness, the positive side must also be controlled in writing.

This is where many disputes begin. The buyer reads "10 mm" as a promise. The mill reads "10 mm per standard tolerance" as a range.

## What to Put on the PO

Write the product form and tolerance route clearly:

> "Hot-rolled stainless plate, dimensions per ASTM A480. Thickness tolerance per applicable plate table. Design minimum thickness not less than [X] mm."

For EN supply:

> "Dimensions per EN 10029, tolerance class [A / C / agreed special class] stated on the order."

For precision strip:

> "Cold-rolled precision strip per ISO 9445 class [P / EP] or agreed mill class. Do not apply sheet tolerance tables."

If the part needs strip precision, buy strip. If it needs plate strength and size, design around plate tolerance. Trying to split the difference usually costs more than choosing correctly at the start.

## What Should Be Clear Before Release?

Before releasing a drawing, ask:

- Is the material being bought as plate, sheet, or strip?
- Does the calculation use nominal thickness or minimum guaranteed thickness?
- Does over-thickness create a weight, fit-up, or machining problem?
- Is the tolerance class named, or only assumed?

When those four answers are clear, hot-rolled plate tolerance stops being a surprise.
