# Which Stainless Grade Holds Up at High Temperature?

High-temperature stainless selection is more than a temperature chart. The same grade can survive a short heat cycle and fail in continuous loaded service. The real questions are: will the surface scale away, will the part creep out of shape, and will the atmosphere attack the alloy in a special way?

That is why [316L](/grades/316l-stainless-steel) is often the wrong "upgrade" for hot service. Molybdenum helps chloride corrosion. It does not make 316L meaningfully better than 304 in oxidation resistance, and low-carbon L grades are weak choices for sustained high-temperature pressure duty.

## The Two Failure Modes

Oxidation is surface loss. Once the protective scale becomes unstable or flakes off during cycling, the section slowly disappears.

Creep is shape loss. Above certain temperatures, a load that is acceptable at room temperature can slowly deform the part over time. This matters for pressure parts, furnace supports, trays, heat exchangers, and anything carrying weight while hot.

Both questions must be answered. A grade that resists scale can still be too weak under load. A grade that carries load can still be wrong in sulfur-bearing or carburizing atmospheres.

## A Practical Grade Ladder

| Grade | Where it usually fits | Main caution |
|---|---|---|
| 304 / 316 | Moderate heat, non-critical exposure | Not a high-temperature pressure answer above the creep range |
| 304H | Sustained hot pressure service where code strength matters | Higher carbon helps creep but changes corrosion/welding logic |
| 321 / 347 | Thermal cycling through sensitization range | Stabilized grades reduce carbide-related risk |
| 309S | Step between 304 and 310S | Useful when 304 is short but 310S is more than needed |
| 310S | High oxidation temperature, furnace hardware | Good scale resistance, not simply "stronger" at all temperatures |
| 253MA / 353MA | Severe heat, cycling, higher oxidation demand | Needs atmosphere review and availability check |

For many real jobs, the decision is between 304H, 321/347, and 310S. The right answer depends less on peak temperature and more on time at temperature, cycling, and load.

## Why Cycling Changes the Answer

Continuous heat lets a stable oxide scale remain in place. Repeated heating and cooling can crack or spall that scale. Once it flakes away, the next cycle has to rebuild it from fresh metal, and section loss accelerates.

If the part cycles through roughly 425-850 °C, sensitization also becomes part of the story. L grades resist carbide precipitation better, but they may not have the creep strength for sustained hot service. Stabilized grades such as 321 or 347 are often better for repeated cycling.

## Atmosphere Can Overrule the Table

Temperature limits assume a broadly oxidizing environment. Real furnaces and process lines may include sulfur, reducing gases, carburizing conditions, salts, or combustion products.

High nickel grades can be vulnerable in sulfur-bearing atmospheres. Carburizing service may require a different alloy logic altogether. Before choosing 310S or 253MA, confirm what the hot gas actually contains. "Air at 900 °C" and "process gas at 900 °C" are not the same specification.

## What to Ask Before Ordering

- Is the temperature continuous or intermittent?
- What is the maximum metal temperature, not the furnace set point alone?
- Does the part carry pressure, weight, or thermal stress while hot?
- Will it cycle through the sensitization range?
- Is the atmosphere oxidizing, reducing, sulfur-bearing, carburizing, or salt-laden?
- Which code basis controls allowable stress, if the part is pressure-related?

These questions prevent the common shortcut: choosing a grade from a single maximum temperature number.

## Choose From the Whole Service Picture

For moderate heat, 304 or 316 may be enough. For sustained hot loaded service, look at 304H, 321, or 347 before assuming an L-grade is safe. For high oxidation temperature, 309S, 310S, 253MA, or 353MA become the ladder.

High-temperature stainless is a service-design choice, not a prettier version of ordinary corrosion selection. If the part is hot, loaded, cycled, or exposed to unusual gas chemistry, the grade needs to be chosen from that whole picture.
