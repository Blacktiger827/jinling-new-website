# Is Your Stainless Steel Food-Grade in the US and the EU?

"Food-grade stainless" is not a single stamp. The US and EU use different regulatory language, and neither system turns a mill certificate into finished-equipment approval.

For buyers, food-grade stainless needs four things to line up: alloy, surface, post-fabrication treatment, and documentation. If one is missing, the metal may be correct while the shipment still fails an audit.

---

## US vs EU: The Document Language

| Market | What buyers usually need | Common mistake |
|---|---|---|
| US | Compliance statement referencing relevant FDA food-contact rules, plus finished-equipment certification where required | Calling stainless "FDA approved" |
| EU | Declaration of Conformity referencing Regulation (EC) 1935/2004 and relevant machinery/material standards | Sending only an FDA-style letter |
| Dairy / hygienic equipment | 3-A, NSF, EN 1672-2, or project-specific hygienic requirements | Treating mill material compliance as equipment certification |

The mill can provide material compliance. The equipment maker is responsible for the finished surface, welds, cleanability, drainage, and any product-level certification.

---

## Layer 1: Alloy

Common food-contact grades are 304/304L and 316/316L. 430 can be suitable for dry, non-aggressive, non-critical food-adjacent components. 201 is not a clean equivalent to 304 for general food-contact work.

Use 304L where the service is mild and cleaning chemistry is controlled. Use 316L when chloride-bearing cleaners, salty foods, acidic foods, marine environments, or higher audit expectations are present.

The grade must be tied to a product standard on the MTC. A letter saying "food grade stainless" without heat number and standard reference is not enough.

---

## Layer 2: Surface

Food-contact stainless must be cleanable. Cleanability depends on roughness, pits, crevices, weld condition, and whether residue can drain.

| Service surface | Typical roughness direction |
|---|---|
| General food-contact sheet or worktop | Ra ≤ 0.8 µm is common |
| Direct washdown or equipment interior | Ra ≤ 0.8 µm or tighter by project |
| Dairy / pharma-adjacent tube and tank interiors | Ra ≤ 0.4 µm or electropolished route |

The regulation may not always name a single Ra value, but the audit will still ask whether the surface is cleanable. If Ra matters, it should be measured and stated in the release documents.

---

## Layer 3: Post-Fabrication Treatment

The mill supplies compliant material. Fabrication can still ruin it.

Welding, grinding, bending, and machining disturb the passive film and can introduce iron contamination. Food-contact equipment normally needs cleaning, pickling where heat tint exists, and passivation after fabrication.

A 316L sheet with a clean MTC is not automatically food-grade after it is welded into a tank. The finished equipment has to be released as a cleanable, passivated, contamination-free surface.

---

## Layer 4: Documentation

A useful food-contact document pack should include:

- MTC with heat number, grade, product standard, and measured chemistry.
- Food-contact compliance letter or Declaration of Conformity for the destination market.
- Surface roughness report where Ra is specified.
- Passivation or post-fabrication treatment record where fabrication is included.
- Finished-equipment certificate where NSF, 3-A, EN 1672-2, or project audit requires it.

The food-contact letter should reference the same heat or shipment as the MTC. Generic letters are weak in an audit.

---

## What Goes Wrong Most Often

| Problem | Why it fails |
|---|---|
| FDA letter sent to EU buyer | Wrong regulatory language |
| "Food grade" letter with no regulation cited | No audit trail |
| MTC not linked to compliance letter | Heat traceability is missing |
| Ra requirement not measured | Surface cleanability cannot be proven |
| Welded equipment shipped without passivation record | Material compliance did not survive fabrication |

Most disputes are not because the alloy is fake. They happen because the document package does not match the destination or the finished use.

---

## Buyer Checklist

Before ordering, ask:

- Which market is the finished equipment entering?
- Is the order material only or finished fabricated equipment?
- Is 304L enough, or does cleaning chemistry justify 316L?
- What surface roughness is required and who measures it?
- Are welds, heat tint removal, and passivation part of the supplier scope?
- Which document wording does the customer or auditor expect?

If the buyer cannot answer the destination-market question, the supplier cannot issue the right compliance language.

## FAQ

**Is "FDA approved stainless steel" real?** Not as a formal material approval phrase. Mills usually issue a compliance statement referencing the relevant FDA food-contact rules.

**Does the EU accept an FDA letter?** Not as a substitute for EU Declaration of Conformity language. EU buyers usually expect Regulation (EC) 1935/2004 wording.

**Is 316L required for all food equipment?** No. 304L is common and acceptable in many mild food-contact uses. 316L is safer where chlorides, acidic foods, or aggressive cleaning are involved.

**Does material compliance certify the finished machine?** No. Finished-equipment certification depends on welds, design, drainage, surface condition, and cleaning route.

**What should a food-grade letter include?** Heat reference, grade, product standard, regulation cited, condition of use, and authorized signature.
