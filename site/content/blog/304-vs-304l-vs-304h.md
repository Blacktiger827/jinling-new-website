# Do You Need 304, 304L, or 304H?

304, 304L, and 304H are not three unrelated grades. They are the same 18-8 stainless family with different carbon control. That small carbon difference decides whether the material is safer for welded corrosion service, ordinary fabrication, or sustained high-temperature duty.

If you remember only one line: use 304L for welded corrosive work, 304H for high-temperature code work, and standard 304 when neither special condition is present.

## What Changes Between Them?

| Variant | Carbon range | Best use | Watch-out |
|---|---:|---|---|
| 304 | <= 0.08% | General fabrication, indoor, mild service | Can sensitize if welded in corrosive service |
| 304L | <= 0.030% | Welded tanks, piping, food equipment, corrosive service | Lower yield floor than standard 304 |
| 304H | 0.04-0.10% | Sustained high-temperature pressure or furnace service | Higher sensitization risk if welded and corrosive |

The chromium and nickel ranges are broadly the same. Carbon is the lever.

## Why 304L Exists

During welding, the heat-affected zone passes through the sensitization range. Carbon can combine with chromium at grain boundaries, leaving nearby metal short of chromium. In corrosive service, those boundaries become attack paths.

304L keeps carbon low enough that this reaction is much slower during normal welding. That is why 304L is the practical default for welded equipment that will see water, humidity, chloride cleaners, food fluids, or mild chemicals.

Dual-certified 304/304L stock is common because modern mills often melt carbon low enough to satisfy both. If the MTC shows carbon at or below 0.030% and the mechanical properties meet the order, dual certification is usually a clean solution.

## Why 304H Exists

High-temperature service asks the opposite of welding service. Above the creep range, some carbon helps hold grain boundaries and improve long-term strength under load.

304H deliberately keeps carbon higher and may require grain-size documentation for code work. It belongs in fired heaters, hot pressure equipment, superheated steam, and other sustained high-temperature duties where allowable stress matters.

Do not choose 304H because it sounds stronger in general. Below the high-temperature window, it adds cost and can create weld-corrosion risk.

## When Standard 304 Is Enough

Standard [304](/grades/304-stainless-steel) remains the right answer for many non-welded or mildly welded parts: indoor architecture, general sheet work, appliance panels, non-corrosive frames, and ordinary formed components.

If the part is welded but stays dry and indoors, 304 may still be acceptable. If the weld sees corrosion, order 304L or dual-certified stock. If the part lives hot under code, review 304H, 321, or 347 instead.

## A Simple Ordering Rule

- Welded plus corrosive: specify 304L or dual-certified 304/304L.
- General room-temperature fabrication: standard 304 is usually fine.
- Sustained service above the code temperature range: check 304H or stabilized grades.
- Hot and corrosive after welding: do not guess inside the 304 family; review [high-temperature stainless](/knowledge-base/high-temperature-stainless-steel) and sensitization risk.

The suffix is not decoration. It tells the supplier which failure mode you are trying to prevent.
