# Which Stainless Steel Works for Water Treatment and Desalination?

Water-treatment stainless should be selected by stream, not by plant name. Potable water, permeate, seawater feed, RO concentrate, chemical cleaning lines, and hot brine do not ask the alloy to do the same job.

The first filter is chloride. The final decision also needs temperature, oxidants, crevices, pressure, and welding route. A plant-level specification such as "marine grade stainless" is too vague for desalination work and too expensive for low-chloride service.

---

## Stream-by-Stream Grade Map

| Water stream or component | Usual starting grade | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Potable / municipal water | 304 or 316L | Low chloride, ambient service |
| Chlorinated dosing, treated wastewater | 316L | Better margin against oxidants and cleaning chemistry |
| Brackish water pretreatment | 316L or 2205 | Depends on chloride, temperature, and maintenance access |
| Ambient seawater RO feed | 2205 as the starting point | 316L has too little crevice margin |
| RO concentrate / reject brine | 2507 or 6Mo | Chloride is concentrated above seawater |
| Hot MSF / MED brine | 2507, 254SMO, titanium, or nickel alloy | Temperature turns chloride into a much harder problem |
| Produced water with H2S / CO2 | 2205, 2507, or nickel alloy by NACE review | Sour-service rules and hardness limits matter |

This table is a starting point, not a release document. Any stream with heat, stagnant zones, gaskets, deposits, or chemical cleaning needs a second review before grade release.

---

## Why 316L Is Not a Seawater Default

316L is useful in water treatment. It is also overused in the wrong water-treatment streams.

For municipal and low-chloride process water, 316L is often the sensible premium over 304. It handles many clean-water, dosing, instrument, and permeate-side duties well. But seawater and concentrated brine are different service windows. A 316L vessel can look economical on the quote and then fail by pitting or crevice corrosion long before the plant reaches its design life.

The practical boundary is this: if chloride is sustained and the surface has crevices, do not make 316L carry the project without corrosion review. For seawater contact, start the discussion at 2205 or higher.

---

## Where 2205 Fits

2205 duplex is the working grade for many brackish and ambient seawater services. It brings higher PREN than 316L, better chloride stress-corrosion-cracking resistance, and much higher yield strength. That strength can reduce wall thickness on pressure equipment when the design code allows it.

2205 is strongest commercially when three things are true:

- Chloride is too high for 316L.
- Temperature is still moderate.
- The fabricator can weld duplex correctly.

The last point matters. Duplex value depends on phase balance and heat control. A poorly welded 2205 assembly can lose the very corrosion margin it was purchased for.

---

## When 2507, 254SMO, or Nickel Alloy Becomes Necessary

RO concentrate, hot brine, seawater heat exchangers, and MSF/MED desalination stages often move beyond 2205. At that point, the choice is usually between super duplex 2507, 6Mo austenitic grades such as 254SMO, titanium, or nickel alloy.

| Grade family | Best use | Watch point |
|---|---|---|
| 2507 super duplex | Hot seawater, brine, pressure service | Welding control and availability |
| 254SMO / 6Mo | Seawater heat exchangers, formed tubes, austenitic fabrication route | High nickel cost and lead time |
| Titanium | Thin-wall seawater heat-exchanger tubes | Different fabrication and procurement route |
| Alloy 625 / nickel alloy | Severe brine, sour service, high consequence duty | Cost and qualification |

The right choice is often not the most corrosion-resistant alloy in the list. It is the alloy that gives enough corrosion margin while the fabricator can still qualify the welding, forming, tube expansion, and documentation route.

---

## Chloride Is Not the Only Variable

Water chemistry is rarely one number. The grade decision should also check:

- Temperature: pitting and crevice risk rise quickly as temperature increases.
- Oxidants: chlorine, hypochlorite, ferric chloride, and cleaning chemicals can push the service above the base-water risk.
- Crevices: gaskets, flanges, deposits, tube sheets, and stagnant zones are usually where failure starts.
- Stress: hot chloride plus residual weld stress can move the issue from pitting into SCC.
- Sour species: H2S and CO2 require NACE / ISO 15156 thinking, beyond PREN comparison.

If any of these are unknown, the material decision is not ready. The order may still be quotable, but the grade should be marked as provisional.

## Fabrication and Release Checks

For water-treatment projects, the material grade and fabrication route should be released together.

| Grade | Fabrication release point |
|---|---|
| 316L | Use low-carbon filler, remove heat tint, passivate after welding |
| 2205 | Use ER2209, control heat input and interpass temperature, verify duplex weld quality |
| 2507 | Use qualified procedures, strict thermal control, and project-specific corrosion/weld testing |
| 254SMO / 6Mo | Use matching filler and confirm post-weld cleaning route |

A certificate showing the correct grade is not enough if welding damages the surface or microstructure. For duplex and super duplex, procedure qualification and release testing should be part of the procurement discussion, not an afterthought.

---

## Selection Shortcut

Use 304 or 316L for clean, low-chloride water. Use 316L or 2205 for brackish and treated wastewater depending on chloride, oxidants, and maintenance access. Use 2205 as the first serious review grade for ambient seawater. Use 2507, 254SMO, titanium, or nickel alloy when temperature, brine concentration, or crevice severity moves beyond 2205.

The cleanest specification habit is stream-by-stream selection. Do not write one stainless grade across the whole plant unless every stream has the same corrosion demand. It almost never does.

## What We Ask Before Quoting

For desalination or water-treatment stainless, we ask for water type, chloride, temperature, pH, oxidants, pressure, whether the part is welded, and whether crevices or stagnant zones are unavoidable. Those answers decide more than the plant name.

If the enquiry only says "marine grade", we stop and ask for the stream. That phrase is too broad to protect either side of the order.

## FAQ

**Is 316L enough for desalination?** It is enough for some low-salinity and permeate-side duties. It should not be treated as the default for seawater feed, concentrated brine, or hot desalination stages.

**Why is 2205 used so often in seawater systems?** It has better chloride and SCC resistance than 316L and much higher yield strength. That combination suits many ambient seawater pressure components.

**When should I choose 2507 instead of 2205?** When the service includes concentrated brine, higher temperature, severe crevices, or high-consequence seawater exposure where 2205's margin is thin.

**Does PREN decide the grade by itself?** No. PREN is a screening tool. Temperature, crevice geometry, welding, and cleaning chemistry can override a simple PREN ladder.

**What documentation should be required?** At minimum, heat-specific chemistry, product standard, MTC, welding procedure where applicable, and any project-required corrosion or NACE documentation.
