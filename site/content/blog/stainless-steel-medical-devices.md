# Which Stainless Steel Belongs in a Medical Device?

"Medical grade stainless" sounds reassuring, but it is not a specification. A device buyer needs to say what the part does: sit in body fluid, cut tissue, carry repeated load, survive autoclave cycles, or simply form a cleanable housing.

Those jobs do not point to one grade.

## Choose by Device Function

| Device role | Common stainless route | Why |
|---|---|---|
| implant or temporary implant | 316L implant route such as ASTM F138 / ISO 5832-1 | tighter cleanliness and corrosion evidence |
| cutting instrument | 420 or 440C route | hardness and edge retention matter |
| reusable handle, forceps body, power-tool part | 17-4 PH or other strength route | strength and machinability matter |
| trays, basins, carts, hospital furniture | 304 or 316L | cleanability and repeated sterilization |
| exterior device housing | 304, 316L, or coated route | appearance, cleaning, and assembly route |

The wrong shortcut is to specify 316L for every medical part. It is excellent where corrosion margin and biological contact matter. It is not the right answer for a cutting edge that needs hardness.

## Implants Need a Different 316L Conversation

[316L](/grades/316l-stainless-steel) for implants is not the same conversation as 316L sheet for a cabinet. Implant routes usually require a specific ASTM or ISO material standard, tighter inclusion control, traceability, and a surface route that supports the finished-device validation.

For long-term orthopedic implants, other materials often take over. For temporary or removable implant applications, implant-grade 316L remains an established route when the device design and regulatory file support it.

The supplier should not claim a finished device is approved. The raw material can support the file; it does not replace device validation.

That distinction protects both sides. The material supplier can provide chemistry, heat traceability, cleanliness evidence, and the agreed condition. Biocompatibility, sterilization validation, packaging, labeling, and regulatory submission sit with the device maker.

## Instruments Have Their Own Logic

Reusable surgical instruments live a hard life: steam sterilization, repeated handling, detergent exposure, and mechanical wear. The best grade depends on whether the part cuts, clamps, bends, or simply holds shape.

Cutting tools need hardness and edge retention, so martensitic grades enter the discussion. Structural handles and machined components may need precipitation-hardening stainless. Trays and basins can often use 304 because the service is cleaning and sterilization, not body-fluid implantation.

Finish matters too. A surface that is too rough retains residue. A surface that is polished badly may look bright but still hold contamination in crevices or around welds. For parts where surface chemistry matters, [electropolishing](/knowledge-base/electropolishing-vs-mechanical-polishing) may be part of the route.

## What the Material Order Should Say

Avoid:

> "medical grade stainless"

Write the function and evidence:

> "316L implant-grade bar to stated ASTM/ISO route, heat traceability and inclusion evidence required."

or:

> "Reusable surgical instrument stainless, grade and hardness condition stated, passivation and cleaning compatibility required."

or:

> "Hospital tray stainless, 304 or 316L, finish and cleaning route stated."

That wording keeps raw material, fabrication, and finished-device responsibility in the right places.
