# When Does Delta E Become a Real Stainless Panel Problem?

Delta E is the number behind a simple complaint: the new panels do not quite match the old ones.

That difference may be tiny in a factory report and obvious in a hotel elevator. It depends on where the panels sit, how they are lit, and whether the second lot is installed beside the first.

## What Delta E Means in Plain Terms

Delta E measures perceived color difference. It combines lightness and color shift into one number.

| Delta E value | What people usually see |
|---|---|
| < 1 | essentially invisible |
| 1-2 | visible only side by side under controlled light |
| 2-3.5 | visible to trained eyes in normal use |
| 3.5-5 | visible to most people |
| > 5 | looks like a different color |

For AFP, PVD, and colored stainless, Delta E is the difference between a controlled release and a soft argument after installation.

## Pick the Tolerance by Use

Do not specify the tightest number by habit. A tighter Delta E target costs time and rejects more lots. Use it where the installed surface deserves it.

| Project | Practical target | Why |
|---|---|---|
| luxury retail, branded appliance fronts | Delta E <= 1 | customers compare surfaces directly |
| elevator cabs, lobbies, appliance panels | Delta E <= 2 | adjacent panels and phased deliveries are common |
| industrial equipment covers | Delta E <= 4 | appearance matters less than function |
| hidden or back-of-house panels | no Delta E target | color match is not a release issue |

Delta E <= 2 is the working baseline for many visible AFP and coated stainless projects. It is strict enough to prevent obvious mismatch without turning every production run into a boutique color exercise.

## Where Drift Comes From

The coating formula is only one source of color drift.

Common causes include:

- resin or coating batch variation
- substrate finish changing from one coil to another
- brushing belt wear changing the way light scatters
- cure temperature or dwell-time drift
- different measurement angle or lighting condition

That is why a physical reference panel matters. A PDF report is useful. A retained sample under the same light is better.

## What to Ask Before Production

Ask three questions:

- What reference sample will every future lot be compared against?
- Which Delta E formula, instrument, illuminant, and viewing angle will be used?
- What happens if the lot measures outside tolerance?

The last answer is the one that tells you how serious the supplier is. "We will pull the lot" is different from "we will discuss it with the customer."

## PO Wording That Works

For visible architectural or appliance work:

> "Color tolerance Delta E <= 2 against approved retained reference sample. Lot-by-lot measurement required. Visual comparison under agreed lighting before shipment."

For premium brand-visible work:

> "Color tolerance Delta E <= 1. Reference panel retained for all repeat orders. Any lot outside tolerance requires written buyer approval before shipment."

Also name the full stack: substrate grade, base finish, coating formula, coating thickness, and protective film. Delta E alone cannot save a project if the base finish changed.
