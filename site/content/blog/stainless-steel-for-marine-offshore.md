# Which Stainless Grade Belongs in Each Marine Zone?

Marine service is not one stainless problem. A guardrail in salt air, a pump skid in the splash zone, a submerged flange, and a sour-service subsea component all need different proof. The grade name is only the beginning; the exposure zone decides how much corrosion margin the metal must carry.

For general coastal work, the question is usually 304 versus 316L. Offshore, that question is too small. Once seawater contact, crevices, biofouling, warm temperature, or H2S appear, the conversation moves to PREN, CPT, CCT, mill qualification, and service envelope.

## Start With the Zone, Not the Grade

| Marine zone | Main risk | Practical grade route |
|---|---|---|
| Marine atmosphere | Salt deposit, humidity, tea staining | 316L with cleaning access |
| Splash zone | Aerated seawater, wet-dry cycling, erosion | 2205 as a starting point; 2507 if warm or critical |
| Tidal zone | Evaporation, biofouling, crevice corrosion | 2205 as a starting point; 2507 when cleaning intervals are long |
| Permanent immersion | Constant chloride, fouling, gasket crevices | 2507 or 254 SMO for dependable margin |
| Sour subsea service | Chloride plus H2S, hardness and heat-treatment limits | Alloy 625 or qualified duplex within ISO 15156 limits |

316L has a legitimate role in marine atmosphere. It does not have a safe universal role in direct seawater contact. The failure point is often not the open face of a plate, but the gasket line, weld toe, bolt pocket, or fouled surface where chloride is trapped.

## Why 316L Fails Offshore So Often

Seawater sits around 19,400 ppm chloride. For 316L, the open-surface pitting margin is already narrow in cool seawater and weak in warm seawater. The crevice margin is worse. Under a gasket or deposit, oxygen disappears and local chemistry becomes far more aggressive than the surrounding water.

That is why a 316L offshore failure can look unfair at first: the material certificate is correct, the grade is real, and the surface may look clean outside the leaking area. The part failed because the local environment was no longer a 316L environment.

## What PREN Does, and What It Cannot Do

PREN is useful because it gives a first-pass view of chloride pitting resistance:

**PREN = %Cr + 3.3 × %Mo + 16 × %N**

Offshore project specifications often set PREN requirements around 35 for direct seawater splash or immersion. When that requirement is written into the job, 316L drops out and [2205 duplex](/grades/2205-duplex-stainless-steel) becomes the first grade to review.

PREN is not the whole decision. It does not prove the weld route, phase balance, crevice behavior, sour-service acceptability, or actual heat performance. For critical offshore work, CPT/CCT data and qualification documents carry the decision further than the grade label.

## When to Move From 2205 to 2507

2205 is a strong marine workhorse, but it is not magic. Upgrade the shortlist to 2507 when any of these are true:

- Seawater temperature is warm enough that crevice margin is thin.
- The part will be immersed or fouled for long periods.
- Cleaning access is poor.
- A shutdown or leak carries high consequence.
- The project specification requires PREN 40 or higher.

254 SMO can be a good alternative in desalination or heavy-wall fabrication where an austenitic route is easier. The important point is not brand loyalty between alloys; it is keeping enough margin after fabrication and geometry have taken their share away.

## Documentation Is Part of the Material Choice

Offshore buyers cannot rely on a simple ASTM A240 line when the service is severe. ASTM chemistry and mechanical properties matter, but they do not qualify the whole production route.

For duplex and super duplex, check whether the mill route is qualified where the project requires it, especially under NORSOK M-650. For sour service, ISO 15156 adds the constraints that actually govern acceptability: heat-treatment condition, hardness, H2S partial pressure, chloride, temperature, and weld HAZ limits.

This is where procurement mistakes become expensive. If the document package is not required at order stage, it may not exist when the EPC or class surveyor asks for it.

## A Cleaner Decision Rule

Use 316L for marine atmosphere where maintenance is believable. Start the review at 2205 for direct seawater splash when the project calls for that corrosion margin. Move to 2507 or 254 SMO for warm, submerged, fouled, or critical seawater service. Use ISO 15156-qualified material when H2S is part of the environment.

Marine and offshore stainless selection is not about choosing the strongest-sounding grade. It is about matching the zone, the geometry, and the paperwork before the part ever touches seawater.
