# When Does Stainless Steel Need Passivation?

Passivation is not a beauty step at the end of production. It is the release step that cleans the surface after fabrication, especially when welding, grinding, machining, forming, or carbon-steel handling has disturbed the passive film.

The common mistake is asking whether the stainless "already passivates in air." It does, if the surface is clean. Fabrication often leaves the surface not clean. Free iron, abrasive residue, fingerprints, weld tint, and shop contamination can all sit between the alloy and the oxygen it needs.

## What Passivation Can and Cannot Do

Passivation removes free iron and helps rebuild a chromium-rich surface. It does not remove heavy weld scale, deep heat tint, paint, oil, or the chromium-depleted layer under a colored weld oxide.

| Surface condition | Is passivation enough? | Better route |
|---|---|---|
| Free iron from tooling or handling | Yes, after pre-cleaning | Citric or nitric passivation |
| Light machining marks on clean stainless | Usually yes | Clean, passivate, verify |
| Weld heat tint, gold to blue | No | Pickle or mechanically remove tint, then passivate |
| Grease, paint, polishing compound | No | Degrease first, then passivate |
| Chloride pitting already active | No | Diagnose grade/environment, then repair or replace |
| Wrong grade for seawater or brine | No | Upgrade alloy; passivation cannot create missing molybdenum |

This boundary matters. A passivated wrong surface can look acceptable at shipment and still fail early in service.

## When to Specify It

Specify passivation when the part has been welded, cut, ground, machined, stamped, or handled around carbon steel. Also specify it when the service is hygienic, coastal, pharmaceutical, food-contact, chloride-bearing, or audit-sensitive.

For decorative sheet that ships as-finished and is not fabricated later, the mill finish may be enough. For anything cut, welded, or assembled after delivery, the final service surface is created by the fabricator, not by the mill. That final surface is the one that should be cleaned and verified.

## Citric or Nitric?

Both can work when the process is controlled. Citric acid is often preferred because waste handling is easier and operator exposure is lower. Nitric acid remains common where legacy specifications require it or where the shop has a validated route for the grade family.

The decision should be based on the grade, contamination level, customer specification, and verification method. It should not be chosen because one acid sounds stronger. Over-aggressive chemistry can attack sensitive grades or leave a dull, uneven surface.

## The Order Matters More Than the Brand of Acid

A clean passivation route usually looks like this:

1. Finish fabrication: cutting, welding, forming, grinding, assembly.
2. Pre-clean: remove oil, grease, polishing compound, fingerprints, and dirt.
3. Pickle only where heat tint or oxide scale is present.
4. Passivate with the specified chemistry and contact time.
5. Rinse thoroughly and dry without recontamination.
6. Verify with the agreed test.

Passivating before welding is a false comfort. The weld destroys the local surface condition and creates a new heat-affected zone. If the service face changes after passivation, the release step needs to follow that change.

## How to Verify the Surface

For routine work, a water-break test and ferroxyl or copper-sulfate spot test are usually more useful than a long certificate with no inspection. The point is simple: prove that free iron is gone and the surface is clean enough to rebuild its passive film.

For critical aerospace, pharmaceutical, or offshore parts, the governing specification may require more formal verification. Follow the project document, but do not let paperwork replace the basic question: was the final surface actually cleaned, rinsed, and protected from recontamination?

## What to Put on the Order

Use a short, explicit note rather than "passivate if needed":

> Passivate after final fabrication and cleaning. Remove visible heat tint before passivation. Verify free-iron removal by agreed field test. Protect from carbon-steel contact after release.

That sentence prevents many arguments. It names the timing, the heat-tint boundary, the verification step, and the handling discipline after passivation.

## Use Passivation for the Right Problem

Passivation is valuable when it is used for the right problem: a contaminated or freshly worked stainless surface that needs its passive film restored. It is not a substitute for pickling, grade selection, or crevice redesign.

If the part will enter chloride, food, pharmaceutical, coastal, or visible architectural service, treat passivation as part of the specification rather than a cleanup favor from the shop.
