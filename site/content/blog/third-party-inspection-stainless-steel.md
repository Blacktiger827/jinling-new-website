# Should You Pay for Third-Party Inspection Before Stainless Steel Ships?

Third-party inspection is not about insulting a supplier. It is about deciding whether an independent witness is cheaper than a bad shipment.

On a first order, a high-value container, a pressure job, a sanitary order, or a supplier route you have not proven yet, inspection before loading often pays for itself. On repeat commodity orders from a supplier with a clean record, it may be unnecessary ceremony.

The decision is risk-based. Not loyalty-based.

## When It Is Usually Worth It

| Situation | Why TPI helps |
|---|---|
| First order with a new supplier | Confirms the route before you build trust on repeat shipments. |
| 316L, duplex, or specialty grade | Grade substitution costs more than inspection. |
| Decorative or mirror surface | Surface defects are easier to reject before container loading. |
| Food, pharma, pressure, or sour service | Documentation and traceability need stronger evidence. |
| Large container value | Inspection cost becomes small compared with replacement risk. |
| Buyer cannot inspect at source | Independent witness closes the distance. |

If the order is small, standard, low-risk, and from a supplier you know well, periodic audits may be better than inspecting every shipment.

## What to Ask the Inspector to Check

Do not send SGS, BV, TÜV, or Intertek a vague brief. A vague brief produces a vague report.

Useful scope:

- MTC and heat-number match
- grade confirmation by PMI/XRF
- dimensions against the exact tolerance standard on the PO
- surface and packaging condition
- quantity and marking
- Ra, gloss, or visual sample comparison if surface-sensitive
- witness testing or 3.2 certificate route where contractually required

For most commercial orders, PMI + dimensions + surface + document review is enough. Full lab chemistry and mechanical testing should be reserved for critical service or disputed material.

## What 3.2 Means

EN 10204 Type 3.2 is more than "extra paperwork." It means an independent party witnesses or verifies the required tests and signs the certificate route.

You normally see it on pressure equipment, PED work, ASME-related projects, sour-service material, or customer contracts that demand independent witnessing. Ordinary commercial 304 sheet normally does not need it.

The important point: name the required certificate type at order stage. Asking for 3.2 after production can create delay or impossibility if the witness points have already passed.

## How to Write a Useful Inspection Brief

A one-page brief is enough if it is specific:

> Material: 316L sheet per ASTM A240.
> Quantity: 2 containers, heat numbers per packing list.
> Inspection: MTC review, heat-number match, PMI on each heat, thickness check per ASTM A480, surface inspection under agreed lighting, packaging photos before loading.
> Report: photos, readings, nonconformance notes, inspector signature.

That tells the inspector what to prove. Without it, the report may say "visual and dimensional inspection passed" while never checking the thing you actually cared about.

## When It Is Probably Not Worth It

Skip or reduce TPI when:

- the supplier has a long clean delivery history
- the grade is commodity and low-risk
- your incoming inspection team can check the same items quickly
- order value is so small that the inspection fee distorts the purchase
- the work is not code-governed and not surface-sensitive

This is not a permanent decision. A supplier can earn reduced inspection after several clean shipments. A supplier can also lose that privilege after one sloppy one.

## TPI Shortcut

Use third-party inspection when the cost of being wrong is bigger than the inspection fee. Put the scope in writing before production ends, inspect before loading, and treat the report as one part of the release file alongside the MTC and receiving record.
