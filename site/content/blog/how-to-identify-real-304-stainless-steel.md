# Is It Really 304 Stainless Steel?

No quick trick proves 304 by itself.

A magnet can warn you. A spot test can help. A certificate can be copied from the wrong heat. The reliable answer comes from two things together: a heat-traceable MTC and a chemistry check on the actual material.

This matters because 201, 304, and some other grades can look nearly identical after polishing. If the price is unusually low, the supplier is new, or the job is corrosion-sensitive, verify before cutting.

## Why the Magnet Test Is Only a Screen

Annealed 304 is usually weakly magnetic or non-magnetic. But cold working can make real 304 respond to a magnet. Deep drawing, bending, rolling, and forming can all create some magnetic response.

Use the magnet this way:

| Magnet result | What it means |
|---|---|
| No or weak pull on annealed sheet | Consistent with 304, not proof. |
| Strong pull on flat annealed sheet | Suspicious; check chemistry. |
| Pull on formed or bent parts | Inconclusive; cold work may be the reason. |
| Strong pull plus very low nickel by PMI | Likely not 304. |

The magnet test is useful because it is fast. It is dangerous only when people treat it as final proof.

## What Chemistry Should 304 Show?

For 304, the field check usually focuses on nickel, chromium, and manganese.

| Grade | Chromium | Nickel | Manganese |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 304 | 18.0-20.0% | 8.0-10.5% | 2.0% max |
| 201 | 16.0-18.0% | 3.5-5.5% | 5.5-7.5% |
| 430 | 16.0-18.0% | 0.75% max | 1.0% max |

The common substitution pattern is simple: nickel goes down, manganese goes up. A handheld XRF gun catches that quickly.

## What Each Test Can Prove

| Method | Good for | Weakness |
|---|---|---|
| Magnet | Fast screen for obvious ferritic or cold-work response | Cannot prove 304. |
| Acid or spot test | Rough field sorting | Operator-dependent, not enough for release. |
| MTC review | Traceability and declared chemistry | Only useful if the heat number matches the material. |
| PMI / XRF | 201 vs 304, 304 vs 316 family check | Cannot verify carbon for 304L. |
| OES or lab chemistry | Formal dispute or L-grade verification | Slower and may leave a small mark. |

For most buyers, the practical route is MTC review before shipment plus PMI on arrival or pre-shipment.

## How to Check the MTC

On the certificate, confirm:

- the heat number appears on both certificate and material
- grade and standard match the PO
- nickel sits inside the 304 range
- manganese is not behaving like a 201 chemistry
- the certificate is issued by a real mill, not a generic trading document

If the certificate and the material heat number do not match, stop. The chemistry on the page may be true for some steel, but not necessarily for the steel in front of you.

For a full certificate walk-through, use [how to read a Mill Test Certificate](/knowledge-base/how-to-read-mill-test-certificate).

## When PMI Is Worth the Cost

PMI is worth doing when:

- the supplier is new
- the quote is far below the market
- the order is 316L, duplex, or other high-value grade
- material has been repacked or traded through multiple hands
- corrosion failure would be expensive
- the customer asks for grade verification records

PMI is not about mistrust as a posture. It is a cheap way to keep the wrong material from entering production.

For PMI limits, see [PMI testing on stainless steel](/knowledge-base/pmi-testing-stainless-steel).

## Verification Shortcut

Use the simple tests to decide whether to investigate. Use chemistry to decide whether to accept.

If you need one clean rule, use this: no heat-number match, no release. No chemistry confirmation on a suspicious lot, no cutting. That is less dramatic than discovering after fabrication that the "304" surface was never 304 at all.
