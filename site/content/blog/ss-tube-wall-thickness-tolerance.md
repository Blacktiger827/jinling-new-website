# Which Tube Wall Reading Should Decide Acceptance?

A tube order can look correct on paper and still arrive too thin for the job. The usual reason is simple: the buyer checked nominal wall, while the standard allows the mill to deliver below nominal.

That is why stainless tube wall tolerance should be discussed from the minimum delivered wall, not from the clean number printed in the catalogue. Seamless, welded, and sanitary tube all answer that question differently.

## Start With the Route, Then Check the Minimum

| Tube route | Normal wall-control reality | Where it fits best |
|---|---|---|
| ASTM A312 seamless pipe | Wider wall spread; seamless commonly allows a larger minus side than welded | Pressure work where the seamless route, code history, or joint factor matters |
| ASTM A312 welded pipe | Usually tighter wall uniformity because it starts from controlled strip | Process lines where consistent wall and lower eccentricity matter |
| ASTM A270 sanitary tube | Fixed absolute wall tolerance, often +/-0.13 mm | Food, dairy, pharma, and hygienic fit-up |
| EN precision seamless tube | D2 or D3 classes can narrow the band | Instrumentation, exchanger, and high-control mechanical uses |

As a common ASTM A312 starting point, seamless pipe may carry a wider wall band such as +22.5 / -12.5%, while welded pipe is commonly held around +/-10%. Those numbers belong in the body of the order, rather than sitting only in the engineer's memory.

The shortcut is not "seamless is better" or "welded is cheaper." Seamless can be the correct pressure-code choice. Welded can be the cleaner dimensional choice. Sanitary tube is judged by hygienic geometry as much as by pressure logic.

## Why Seamless and Welded Do Not Behave the Same

Seamless pipe starts from a solid billet. The wall is formed while the steel is hot, moving, and being pierced over tooling. That process can leave eccentricity around the circumference: one side reads thicker, the opposite side reads thinner.

Welded tube starts from strip. The strip gauge has already been controlled in a rolling mill before it is formed into a tube and welded. That does not make every welded tube suitable for every duty, but it explains why its wall readings are usually steadier.

For pressure design, the engineer still has to include mill undertolerance. If the calculated minimum wall is 2.55 mm, ordering a nominal 2.77 mm wall does not automatically work. A minus tolerance can pull the legal delivered wall below the design minimum. The order should be sized from the minimum allowed wall, not the optimistic nominal.

## What to Write on the PO

If wall matters, the purchase order needs more than grade, OD, and schedule.

Use language like this:

> "Tube per ASTM A312 / SA-312, 316L, welded or seamless as stated. Nominal wall to include code mill undertolerance. Minimum delivered wall not less than [X] mm. Wall readings to be reported at both ends, four clock positions per end."

For sanitary tube, add the surface side:

> "ASTM A270 sanitary tube. Wall tolerance per standard. Internal weld condition and Ra requirement to be inspected with wall readings."

If the line is sensitive, do not use "tight tolerance" as a phrase. Write the tolerance class, the minimum wall, and the measurement method.

## How to Inspect Without Creating an Argument

One reading at the tube end is not enough. Measure at four clock positions: 0, 90, 180, and 270 degrees. Do that at both ends of the length. A ball-anvil micrometer gives a direct end reading; UT is useful when the tube cannot be cut or when many lengths need to be screened.

Record the lowest number, because that is the number that matters for release.

Accept the lot when the lowest reading meets the written standard or PO limit. Hold it when the reading is below the PO minimum, or when eccentricity is so large that the tube cannot meet the intended process even if the average wall looks fine.

## What Should the Buyer Refuse to Leave Open?

Wall tolerance is not a small-print standards issue. It decides whether a tube can pass pressure calculation, hygienic fit-up, exchanger expansion, or instrumentation control.

The safer habit is to ask three questions before the PO is released:

- What is the minimum wall the design really needs?
- Which tube route can hold that minimum with the least receiving risk?
- How will we measure the wall when the material arrives?
