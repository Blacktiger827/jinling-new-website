# Should You Upgrade to 316L, or Fix the Surface First?

When 304 stains too early, the reflex is to move the next order to 316L. Sometimes that is the right call. Sometimes it is an expensive way to avoid asking a sharper question: did the alloy fail, or did the surface fail?

Visible architectural panels, kitchen fronts, elevator interiors, hospital furniture, and decorative equipment often fail from surface contamination, weak passivation, aggressive cleaning residue, or chloride sitting in a brushed grain. If the attack is surface-driven and the environment is not true immersion, a better engineered surface may solve the problem before the alloy has to change.

## What Super Finish Is

Super Finish is a passivation-enhanced No.4, hairline, or satin-brushed surface. The appearance stays in the same visual family. The difference is the finishing control behind it: cleaner polishing, tighter handling, and a passivation route tuned to a textured visible surface.

The aim is simple. Keep the look buyers want, but reduce the weak surface sites where tea staining and early pitting normally begin.

## How to Read the Six-Times Claim

Jinling's Super Finish route is marketed as giving up to six times the corrosion-resistance reserve compared with the base-material benchmark used in Jinling's ASTM, BS, and JIS reference comparisons. That is a useful claim, but it has a narrow job: it describes a controlled surface route, not a promise that every 304 panel will last six times longer on every site.

The claim belongs only when the substrate, brushing route, passivation, protective film, handling, and service exposure are all kept inside the agreed release condition. It does not replace the right grade for immersion, trapped brine, hot chloride, welded critical assemblies, or unknown cleaning chemistry.

## When the Surface Route Makes Sense

| Project condition | Super Finish on 304 can make sense | 316L or higher is safer |
|---|---|---|
| Coastal air on visible panels | Yes, if not splash or immersion | Yes for harsher C4/C5 exposure |
| Kitchen or public interior with cleaning chemicals | Often yes | If chlorides are heavy or hygiene spec requires it |
| Tea staining on decorative brushed panels | Often yes | If pitting is deep or environment is severe |
| Direct seawater splash | No | 2205 or higher should be reviewed |
| Process chloride, brine, CIP loops | No | 316L, duplex, or higher by chemistry |
| Welded critical assemblies | Only if finish can be restored after fabrication | Alloy and weld cleanup must lead |

The line is exposure. Airborne salt and cleaning residue are surface-life problems. Immersion, brine, hot chloride, and crevices are alloy-life problems.

## Why 316L Is Not Always the First Answer

316L adds molybdenum, and molybdenum improves chloride resistance. That is real. But if the early stain came from embedded iron, polishing residue, a damaged passive film, or poor post-fabrication handling, the 316L upgrade may hide the process problem instead of fixing it.

In visible work, surface quality affects the first complaint the owner sees. A clean, well-passivated 304 panel can outperform a poorly handled 316L panel in mild service. That does not make 304 the better alloy. It means workmanship and surface chemistry still count.

## When 316L, 2205, or Higher Must Lead

Do not use Super Finish as a substitute for the right grade in sustained chloride service. If seawater sits on the part, if the geometry traps brine, if the surface runs hot, or if the component is service-critical, alloy selection must lead the decision.

For coastal direct exposure, [is 304 enough near the coast](/knowledge-base/is-304-enough-for-coastal-use) is the better first filter. For true saltwater service, move to [saltwater stainless selection](/knowledge-base/saltwater-stainless-steel) before discussing decorative finish.

## How to Decide Without Guessing

Ask four questions:

- Is the complaint cosmetic tea staining, or is there real pitting depth?
- Is the exposure airborne salt and cleaning chemistry, or direct chloride solution?
- Will the surface be welded, cut, or drilled after the finish is supplied?
- Can the project accept 304 base material if the surface route is documented?

If the answer points to surface condition, Super Finish can be the more economical fix. If the answer points to chloride chemistry and bulk corrosion margin, the alloy upgrade is not optional.

## What to Put on the Order

For a surface-led route:

> 304 or 304L with Jinling Super Finish No.4/HL, approved sample retained, final protective film and handling controls, no carbon-steel contact after passivation.

For an alloy-led route:

> 316L, 2205, or higher grade per service environment, with finish and passivation specified after final fabrication.

The wording matters because the failure often enters after the sheet leaves the finishing line. Cutting, welding, drilling, and handling can undo a good surface if the downstream process is not controlled.

## Let the Failure Mode Spend the Money

Upgrade the alloy when the environment demands more chloride margin. Fix the surface first when the problem is contamination, weak passivation, or visible tea staining in mild-to-moderate exposure.

The best specification is not always the highest alloy. It is the one that names the real failure mode before spending money in the wrong place.
