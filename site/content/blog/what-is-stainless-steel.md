# What Makes Stainless Steel Stainless?

Stainless steel is not one material. It is a family of iron alloys that contain enough chromium to form a thin protective film on the surface. That film is why stainless resists rust better than ordinary carbon steel.

The word "stainless" can be misleading. It means corrosion-resistant, not corrosion-proof. A kitchen sink, a coastal facade, and a seawater pump may all use stainless, but they should not all use the same grade.

## The Chromium Threshold

Steel becomes stainless when it contains about 10.5% chromium or more. Chromium reacts with oxygen to form a thin chromium-oxide passive film. The film is transparent, so the metal still looks metallic. It is also self-repairing when oxygen is available.

That last phrase matters. If oxygen cannot reach the surface, such as inside a tight crevice or under a deposit, the film can fail. If chloride is too strong for the grade, the film can pit. Stainless works when the grade and environment still support the film.

## The Main Families

| Family | Common grades | What it is good at |
|---|---|---|
| Austenitic | 304, 316L | General corrosion resistance, forming, welding |
| Ferritic | 430, 409 | Lower cost, magnetic behavior, indoor and exhaust service |
| Martensitic | 410, 420 | Hardness and wear |
| Duplex | 2205, 2507 | Strength plus chloride resistance |
| PH | 17-4PH | Very high-strength machined parts |

The family gives the first clue. The exact grade finishes the decision.

## Why Alloying Elements Matter

Chromium starts the passive film. Nickel helps make grades such as [304](/grades/304-stainless-steel) formable and tough. Molybdenum helps [316L](/grades/316l-stainless-steel) resist chloride better than 304. Nitrogen adds strength and pitting resistance in duplex and nitrogen-alloyed grades. Carbon can add hardness or hot strength, but it can also create weld-corrosion risk.

That is why two stainless grades can look similar and behave very differently in service.

## Why Stainless Still Rusts

Rust usually means one of four things happened:

- The environment exceeded the grade's corrosion margin.
- Carbon-steel contamination was left on the surface.
- Welding damaged the passive film or sensitized the heat-affected zone.
- Crevices, deposits, or stagnant water blocked oxygen.

The fix depends on the cause. Passivation helps free iron contamination. Pickling helps heat tint. A higher grade helps chloride overload. Better joint design helps crevices.

## How to Start a Grade Choice

Start with the environment, then fabrication, then strength:

1. Is chloride, seawater, acid, heat, or outdoor exposure present?
2. Will the part be welded, deep drawn, polished, or heat treated?
3. Does the part need unusual strength, hardness, or magnetic behavior?

Those three answers usually move you toward the right family before you compare exact grade names.

## The Simple Rule

Stainless steel works because a very thin passive film keeps the surface protected. Choose the grade by asking what could break that film in the real service.

If the answer is mild indoor use, [304](/grades/304-stainless-steel) may be enough. If the answer is chloride, welding, seawater, or high load, the word stainless is only the starting point.
