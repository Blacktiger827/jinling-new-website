# Is 304 Stainless Enough Near the Coast?

Sometimes, but not as often as buyers hope. [304](/grades/304-stainless-steel) is a strong everyday stainless for indoor and mild outdoor use. Near the coast, its problem is simple: it has no molybdenum, so its chloride margin runs out quickly when salt stays on the surface.

The real question is not the distance from the sea on a map. It is whether salt lands on the part, whether moisture keeps it there, and whether anyone can clean the surface before staining and pitting begin.

## Where 304 Can Still Work

304 can be reasonable for protected interiors, inland coastal locations, temporary fixtures, or parts where light tea staining can be cleaned without major consequence. It also works better where rain can wash the surface and the finish is smooth enough not to trap salt.

It becomes a weak choice when the surface is sheltered from rain, close to breaking waves, exposed to humid night air, brushed with deep grain, or difficult to replace. Hidden clips and fasteners are often the first failure, because they see tight crevices and almost no cleaning.

## A Practical Coastal Grade Map

| Coastal condition | Better starting point | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Protected indoor space near coast | 304 or 316L depending on appearance risk | Low salt load, easier cleaning |
| Outdoor but inland, rain-washed | 304 may work; 316L if appearance matters | Salt deposit is intermittent |
| Exposed exterior within salt-air zone | 316L | Mo improves pitting and tea-stain margin |
| Sheltered exterior faces, fixings, clips | 316L as the starting point | Salt sits longer in hidden pockets |
| Splash, tidal wetting, direct seawater | 2205 or higher | 316L runs out of immersion margin |

This table deliberately avoids a false exact boundary. Wind, humidity, shade, maintenance, and local seawater temperature can move the grade decision more than a neat distance number.

## Why Fasteners Deserve Their Own Check

A 316L panel with 304 fasteners is not a 316L assembly. In coastal air, the small fastener sits in a crevice, holds moisture, and may be galvanically disadvantaged against the larger stainless surface around it.

That is how clean panels end up with rust streaks from the fixing points. The visible sheet gets specified carefully; the hidden hardware gets value-engineered. The building then advertises the shortcut every time it rains.

Use matching 316 stainless fasteners for coastal 316L assemblies, and move higher where direct seawater or severe crevice exposure is present.

## Can Maintenance Save 304?

Fresh-water rinsing can extend life by removing chloride deposits before they concentrate. It works best when the surface is open, smooth, and easy to reach.

The limitation is human. Maintenance schedules slip. Hidden faces are ignored. Owners change. If the part only survives because someone must rinse it every two weeks without fail, the material is probably too lean for the real project.

## Finish Still Matters

Rough brushed finishes hold salt longer than smoother 2B, BA, mirror, or well-polished surfaces. Passivation after fabrication helps remove free iron and reset the surface. Both are worth specifying.

But finish is not a substitute for alloy. A smooth 304 surface near heavy salt exposure may stain later, but it is still 304. If the environment needs 316L or duplex, the finish cannot carry the missing chemistry.

## The Line I Would Draw

Use 304 only when the coastal exposure is mild, cleanable, and low consequence. Let 316L lead the review for exposed coastal architecture and equipment. Bring duplex into the shortlist when the part sees splash, trapped seawater, or direct marine contact.

Most coastal failures come from treating 304 as "good enough because it is stainless." Stainless is not the spec. The salt load is.
