# What Does Food-Grade Stainless Actually Need to Prove?

"Food grade" is useful in conversation and dangerous on a purchase order. It can mean the right stainless alloy, a cleanable finish, a passivated weld, an NSF route, a 3-A route, or simply a seller trying to sound safe.

For real equipment, food-grade stainless needs three things to line up: alloy, surface, and finished-equipment compliance.

In the United States, FDA food-contact rules and NSF equipment routes do not turn a sheet or coil into a finished-equipment approval. They define what the finished article must be able to support. The same practical boundary applies to 3-A and EHEDG routes.

## The Alloy Is the Starting Point

[304](/grades/304-stainless-steel) covers a large share of food-contact work: prep tables, sinks, conveyors, hoppers, tanks, and general food equipment where chloride and acidity are moderate.

[316L](/grades/316l-stainless-steel) becomes the safer route when the surface regularly sees salt, brine, dairy chemistry, acidic sauces, chlorinated cleaners, coastal air, or wet service that does not dry quickly.

430 can be acceptable in dry, low-risk food-adjacent uses such as some appliance skins or trays. It should not be quietly used for welded, wet, or CIP-cleaned contact surfaces.

## The Surface Has to Be Cleanable

A food-contact surface is judged by what residue can stay behind after cleaning. That is why surface roughness matters. A finish name such as 2B, No.4, or BA is helpful, but it does not replace a measured Ra target when hygiene risk is high.

| Food-contact situation | Practical surface route | What to ask for |
|---|---|---|
| dry contact or low-risk panels | 2B or controlled No.4 | cleanable finish, no dirt-trap defects |
| wet food contact | 2B or polished route | Ra target and weld clean-up |
| dairy, brine, acidic product | 316L with controlled finish | alloy margin plus measured surface |
| hygienic process / sanitary tube | sanitary route, often polished | Ra record, weld/passivation evidence |

The finish after fabrication matters more than the finish on the incoming sheet. Cutting, welding, bending, grinding, and field repair can all damage the passive surface.

## Standards Sit at Equipment Level

The mill can provide the material evidence: MTC, grade, chemistry, thickness, finish route, and any measured roughness ordered.

It normally cannot certify a finished table, sink, valve, or process skid to NSF 51, 3-A, EHEDG, or a customer hygiene standard. That file belongs to the equipment maker and the final design.

The clean split is:

- mill: material chemistry, heat traceability, surface/roughness evidence where ordered
- fabricator: welds, geometry, drainage, passivation, final roughness
- equipment owner or certifier: compliance of the finished equipment route

## What the PO Should Say

Avoid:

> "food-grade stainless"

Write the real requirement:

> "304 2B food-contact sheet, MTC required, surface protected for fabrication."

or:

> "316L food-contact tube, wet chloride service, Ra target stated, passivation record required after fabrication."
