# Which Stainless Steel Works Against H₂SO₄, HCl, and HNO₃?

Acid service is where generic "stainless is corrosion resistant" language becomes dangerous. Nitric acid, sulfuric acid, hydrochloric acid, caustic, and mixed cleaning streams do not reward the same alloy.

The grade decision starts with the acid identity, then concentration, temperature, contamination, and upset conditions. A grade that survives the steady state can still fail during a short concentration spike.

---

## First Split: Oxidizing or Reducing?

| Chemistry | Stainless behavior | Practical reading |
|---|---|---|
| Nitric acid | Oxidizing; supports the passive film | 304L / 316L often work in defined ranges |
| Dilute sulfuric acid | Reducing; strips the passive film | 316L only has a narrow low-temperature window |
| Hydrochloric acid | Reducing plus chloride | Common stainless usually fails quickly |
| Hot caustic | SCC risk rises with concentration and temperature | Nickel alloys or special review may be needed |
| Hydrofluoric acid | Attacks the passive film directly | Do not specify stainless as the solution |

This split matters more than alloy prestige. 316L is not automatically better than 304L in nitric acid. 316L is also not a serious answer for most hydrochloric acid service.

---

## Sulfuric Acid

Sulfuric acid changes behavior with concentration. Dilute sulfuric is aggressive to common stainless. Cold concentrated sulfuric can sometimes keep stainless passive, but that margin collapses with temperature or dilution zones.

| Service condition | Sensible material direction |
|---|---|
| Very dilute H₂SO₄, cool, controlled | 316L may be considered with monitoring |
| 5–30% sulfuric or elevated temperature | Alloy 20 or nickel alloy review |
| Mid-concentration hot sulfuric | Common stainless is normally out |
| Cold concentrated sulfuric | 316L may work only if dilution and heat are controlled |

The dangerous point is the inlet, dilution zone, or cleaning upset. A tank may survive average chemistry and fail where the acid first mixes.

---

## Hydrochloric Acid

Hydrochloric acid pushes the decision out of ordinary stainless very quickly. Even low concentrations can combine uniform corrosion with pitting and crevice attack.

For HCl, the review usually moves toward nickel alloys such as C-276 or C-22, or toward lined equipment. Titanium may work in some dilute oxidizing conditions, but it is not a blanket HCl answer.

If the stream contains HCl above trace contamination, do not approve 304 or 316L from a general corrosion table. Use actual concentration, temperature, oxidizing contaminants, and upset chemistry.

---

## Nitric Acid

Nitric acid is the exception that makes stainless look easy. It is strongly oxidizing and can support the passive film.

304L is often a good nitric-service grade within defined concentration and temperature limits. 316L does not automatically add much in pure nitric acid, though it may help when trace chlorides are present. For welded nitric service, the L-grade matters because sensitization can turn the heat-affected zone into the weak point.

Fuming or very concentrated nitric is a different service and may require high-silicon stainless, glass-lined equipment, or other specialist materials.

---

## PREN Helps Less in Acid Than Buyers Expect

PREN is useful for neutral chloride pitting. Acid service is broader. It includes uniform dissolution, oxidizing/reducing behavior, temperature effects, contamination, and local concentration.

Use PREN to understand chloride margin. Do not use PREN to approve acid service by itself.

| Question | Better basis |
|---|---|
| Chloride pitting in near-neutral water | PREN, CPT, CCT |
| Sulfuric acid attack | Isocorrosion data and coupon testing |
| HCl service | Nickel-alloy or lining review |
| Nitric welded equipment | L-grade, sensitization, Huey/IGC testing where required |
| Mixed cleaning chemicals | Worst-case chemistry and exposure time |

---

## Specify for the Upset Case

The most expensive acid-service mistake is specifying for normal operation and ignoring the upset.

Ask these five questions before approving the grade:

- What is the highest concentration the alloy will see?
- What is the highest temperature at that concentration?
- How often does the upset happen?
- Are chlorides, oxidants, or reducing contaminants present?
- Will the surface have welds, crevices, deposits, or stagnant zones?

If operations says "only for fifteen minutes", ask how often. A quarterly upset can decide service life.

---

## Selection Shortcut

Use 304L/316L for defined oxidizing nitric service. Be cautious with 316L in sulfuric; its working window is narrow. Move out of ordinary stainless quickly for hydrochloric or hydrofluoric service. Use Alloy 20, C-276/C-22, 6Mo, nickel, titanium, or lining only after the actual stream is named.

The PO should include acid identity, concentration range, temperature range, contamination, and upset condition. Without those, the grade callout is not really a specification.

## FAQ

**Is 316L always better than 304L in acid?** No. 316L helps in some chloride and sulfuric windows, but it does not automatically outperform 304L in nitric acid.

**Can stainless handle hydrochloric acid?** Common 304/316 stainless generally should not be specified for meaningful HCl service. Move to specialist alloy or lining review.

**Why does nitric acid suit stainless better?** It is oxidizing, so it supports the chromium passive film instead of stripping it.

**Should I use PREN for acid selection?** Only as part of the picture. PREN is mainly for chloride pitting, not full acid-service approval.

**What data should I give the supplier?** Acid name, concentration, temperature, contaminants, pressure, cleaning cycles, upset chemistry, and whether the part is welded.

For chloride-driven pitting, use [PRE value selection](/knowledge-base/pre-value-stainless-steel-selection). For stress-driven cracking, use [stress corrosion cracking](/knowledge-base/stress-corrosion-cracking-scc).
