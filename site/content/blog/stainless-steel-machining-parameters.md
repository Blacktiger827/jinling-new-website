# How Do You Machine Stainless Without Work-Hardening It?

Most stainless machining problems start before the parameter table is wrong. They start when the insert rubs instead of cuts. The surface work-hardens, the next pass meets a harder skin, and the tool fails faster the more gently the operator tries to save it.

The cure is not a magic speed number. It is a setup discipline: sharp positive-rake tooling, enough feed to make a real chip, coolant aimed at the chip root, and insert changes before wear turns into burnishing.

## Start With the Grade

| Grade / condition | Turning speed starting range | Setup note |
|---|---:|---|
| 304 annealed | 120-180 m/min | Good baseline, but work-hardens quickly |
| 316L annealed | 90-140 m/min | Reduce speed slightly; tougher chip |
| 430 annealed | 120-200 m/min | Easier chip break, less work hardening |
| 2205 duplex | 60-100 m/min | Higher force, use stronger setup and feed |
| 17-4PH aged | 40-80 m/min | Treat as hard stainless, not ordinary 304 |

These are starting points under flood coolant. The machine, insert grade, rigidity, and delivery condition still decide the final number.

## Feed Is Not Where to Save a Worn Tool

On austenitic stainless, very light feed is dangerous. Below minimum chip thickness, the tool smears the surface and creates a hardened layer. The next pass then cuts a harder material with a duller edge.

For turning 304 or 316L, keep finishing feed high enough to cut cleanly, often around 0.12-0.15 mm/rev minimum. For roughing, 0.2-0.4 mm/rev is common when the setup is rigid. If wear starts changing the sound or chip, replace the insert. Do not compensate by dropping feed into a rubbing pass.

## Tool Geometry Comes Before Coating

Positive rake matters on stainless. A sharp edge with 5-15 degrees positive rake lowers cutting force and reduces smearing. Stainless-specific chip breakers also matter because long stringy chips can scratch the surface or wrap the tool.

Coatings help, but they cannot rescue the wrong geometry. TiAlN is a good general coating for 304 and 316L. Duplex and harder grades may need tougher carbide or AlCrN-type coatings. A worn insert that would still cut carbon steel can be unusable on stainless.

## Coolant Has to Reach the Cut

Flood coolant is part of the cutting system. It cools the edge, reduces built-up material, and moves chips away before they re-cut the surface.

For ordinary stainless turning and milling, use a stainless-compatible, chloride-free coolant and point the nozzle at the chip formation zone rather than the tool body. Poor aim can look like enough coolant from the outside and still leave the cutting edge running hot.

Avoid chloride-bearing cutting fluids when the part will enter food, coastal, or corrosion-sensitive service. Residue trapped in machined marks can become the first corrosion site.

## The Checks Before Production

- Confirm delivery condition: annealed, cold-worked, hardened, or aged.
- Use a sharp positive-rake insert and a chip breaker matched to the feed.
- Keep feed high enough to cut under the work-hardened skin.
- Use enough depth of cut that the finish pass is genuinely cutting.
- Aim coolant at the chip root and keep chips out of the cut.
- Change inserts early on stainless instead of nursing a worn edge.

## The Shop-Floor Rule

Stainless rewards decisive cutting. It punishes timid cutting. If the operation starts to rub, every next pass gets harder.
