# Which Stainless Grade Belongs on Each Vehicle Zone?

Cars do not use stainless in one way. The exhaust manifold is fighting thermal fatigue. The muffler is fighting acidic condensate. A visible trim strip is fighting fingerprints and road-salt staining. A fuel or EV component may need a different corrosion margin again.

That is why the automotive answer is not "use 304 because it is better." In many exhaust positions, ferritic stainless is the better engineering answer.

## Start With the Vehicle Zone

| Vehicle zone | Usual stainless route | Why it works |
|---|---|---|
| hot exhaust manifold / close-coupled converter | 409 or 441 | lower thermal expansion and better thermal-fatigue behavior than 304 |
| mid-pipe and muffler | 409, 439, or 441 | balances condensate corrosion, heat, and cost |
| visible tailpipe trim | 304, sometimes 316L | appearance and polish matter more |
| interior decorative trim | 430 or 304 | dry service, finish quality, and cost drive the choice |
| fuel, coastal trim, selected EV parts | 304, 316L, or program-specific route | corrosion, cleanliness, joining, and validation matter more than exhaust-style cost logic |

The exhaust system usually carries the largest stainless share. It is also where grade names are most easily misunderstood.

## Why Ferritic Grades Dominate Exhaust

Ferritic exhaust grades such as 409, 439, and 441 are not cheap substitutes for 304. They suit the job because they expand less during heat cycling, avoid chloride stress-corrosion cracking behavior common to austenitic grades, and remove nickel cost from high-volume vehicle programs.

409 is the volume workhorse. It can show a rust-colored surface blush in salt-belt service, but that does not automatically mean the system has failed. The question is wall loss, weld condition, and whether the component still meets its design life.

439 and 441 add more chromium reserve. 441 also brings stronger high-temperature stabilization, which is why it is often reviewed for manifolds, converter shells, and hotter front-end sections. If a 409 weld area is cracking after repeated thermal cycles, simply asking for "better stainless" is too vague. The review should look at grade, filler, heat input, geometry, and thermal restraint together.

## Where 304 and 316L Make Sense

[304](/grades/304-stainless-steel) belongs where formability, polish quality, or visible appearance matters more than exhaust heat-cycle economics. Tailpipe tips, premium trims, decorative pieces, and some formed parts often land there.

[316L](/grades/316l-stainless-steel) is not a default automotive grade. It earns its place when chloride, fuel chemistry, coastal exposure, or a customer-facing appearance requirement makes the extra corrosion margin worth paying for. On EV battery or thermal-management parts, the final program specification should control cleanliness, joining, leak testing, and corrosion evidence; the grade name alone is too thin.

430 sits in a different corner again: dry interior trim, appliance-like panels, and decorative parts where ferritic magnetism and lower cost are acceptable. It should not be quietly moved into wet or salt-exposed service just because it is stainless.

## What the Drawing Should Say

An automotive stainless line item should not rely on the grade name alone. Write the part by service condition:

- hot-zone exhaust: grade, thickness, stabilizing requirement, weld/filler route, and heat-cycle expectation
- rear exhaust: condensate and salt exposure, coating or surface expectation, and acceptance for surface discoloration
- visible trim: finish, grain or polish direction, protective film, and cosmetic acceptance condition
- EV or fuel-related part: grade, cleanliness, edge condition, and corrosion evidence required by the program
