# Why Does Stainless Steel Lose Its Passive Film?

Stainless does not stay clean because it is noble all the way through. It stays clean because the surface carries a very thin chromium-oxide film. When that film is intact and oxygen can reach it, small scratches recover quickly. When the film is blocked, contaminated, burned, or attacked by chloride, the steel underneath behaves much more like ordinary metal.

That is why "stainless rusted" is not a diagnosis. It is only the symptom. The useful work is figuring out what stopped the film from repairing itself.

## What the Film Is Doing

The passive film is a transparent chromium-oxide layer, usually only 1-10 nm thick. It forms naturally when stainless contains enough chromium, roughly 10.5% or more, and the surface has access to oxygen.

This film is not a coating added by the mill. It grows from the alloy itself. A clean 304, 316L, or duplex surface will rebuild the film after light damage, provided the local environment allows oxygen and does not overwhelm the grade.

[Passivation treatment](/knowledge-base/stainless-steel-passivation-guide) helps after fabrication because it removes free iron and surface contamination. It does not make a poor grade suitable for the wrong chloride service, and it does not remove weld heat tint by itself.

## Why the Film Fails

| What changed at the surface | What you may see | Better fix |
|---|---|---|
| Free iron from tools, chains, grinding dust | Orange spots on otherwise clean stainless | Clean, passivate, separate stainless tools |
| Weld heat tint | Rust or staining along the weld zone | Pickle or mechanically remove tint, then passivate |
| Chloride exceeds grade margin | Pitting near coast, saltwater, cleaners, brine | Upgrade grade or reduce chloride exposure |
| Crevice blocks oxygen | Rust under gaskets, bolts, deposits, lap joints | Redesign, seal, drain, or use higher alloy |
| Sensitization after heat exposure | Grain-boundary attack near welds or hot zones | Use L-grade or stabilized grade; prevent at design stage |
| Low pH or aggressive chemistry | General attack or rapid dulling | Recheck chemical compatibility before blaming finish |

The same brown mark can come from very different causes. Passivating a heat-tinted weld without removing the tint leaves the chromium-depleted layer in place. Upgrading alloy will not fix carbon-steel contamination in the shop. A better surface finish will not save a tight chloride crevice forever.

## Why Oxygen Access Matters

The passive film heals only where oxygen can participate. On an open surface, a scratch can repassivate quickly. Under a gasket, under scale, inside a dead leg, or beneath marine deposits, the local pocket may run out of oxygen. Then chloride and acidity concentrate while the film loses its ability to recover.

That is the quiet reason crevice corrosion is so common. The bulk environment may look mild, but the trapped pocket no longer behaves like the bulk environment.

## Where Alloy Chemistry Helps

Chromium creates the baseline film. Molybdenum and nitrogen help the film resist chloride breakdown and repassivate after local attack. That is why [316L](/grades/316l-stainless-steel) handles coastal service better than [304](/grades/304-stainless-steel), and why [2205 duplex](/grades/2205-duplex-stainless-steel) or super duplex becomes necessary when saltwater is direct and sustained.

PREN is useful as a screening number, but it does not replace judgment. A higher PREN surface can still fail inside a bad crevice. A smooth, passivated 304 can still fail in seawater. Chemistry helps only when the environment is inside its real working range.

## How to Read the Failure Before Repairing It

- Rust spots scattered across new parts usually point to free iron contamination.
- Rust following weld color usually means heat tint or sensitization, not a bad cleaning cloth.
- Pits across open surfaces usually mean chloride has exceeded the alloy margin.
- Rust at gaskets, washers, threads, and overlaps usually means oxygen-starved crevice attack.
- Rust after fabrication often needs a process fix, not a stronger acid alone.

The cheapest repair is often the one that starts slowly: look at the stain, locate the geometry, confirm the grade, and ask what changed at the surface. Only then choose passivation, pickling, redesign, or alloy upgrade.

## Small Film, Specific Failure

The passive film is thin, but it is not fragile when the design respects it. Keep the surface clean, remove heat tint properly, avoid carbon-steel contamination, design out stagnant pockets, and choose a grade with enough chloride margin.

When the film fails, do not treat every brown mark as the same problem. Stainless usually tells you what went wrong if you look at where the rust starts.
