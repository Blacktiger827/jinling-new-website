# How Do You Write a Coastal Stainless Spec That Blocks Substitution?

Coastal stainless specs fail in two places. The grade is wrong, or the written spec is too loose to stop substitution.

This article is about the second problem. If the design decision is already "316L exterior," the spec still has to prevent 304, standard 316, low-Mo 316L, or a mismatched finish from quietly arriving on site.

For the grade-choice question itself, see [is 304 enough for coastal use](/knowledge-base/is-304-enough-for-coastal-use).

## Write the Exposure First

Do not write "coastal" as if every coast is the same. State the exposure zone or at least the intended severity:

- sheltered inland coastal air
- direct marine aerosol
- splash zone
- pool or warm chloride atmosphere
- transition zone between exterior and interior lobby

The transition zone is easy to miss. Revolving doors, canopy soffits, open-air lift lobbies, and breezeways may look "interior" on a schedule while behaving like exterior stainless in service.

## The Clause Needs More Than a Grade Name

| Spec item | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| UNS / EN grade | Prevents vague "316 type" substitution. |
| Minimum PREN | Controls actual heat chemistry instead of trusting the grade label alone. |
| MTC per heat | Lets the inspector verify Cr, Mo, N, and C. |
| Finish requirement | Rough or mismatched finishes raise staining risk. |
| Weld treatment | Heat tint is a corrosion starting point near the coast. |
| No-substitution language | Blocks cheaper alternatives without written approval. |

The grade name is the start of the clause, not the whole clause.

## Sample 316L Coastal Clause

Use language like this:

> Exposed exterior stainless elements shall be ASTM A240 UNS S31603 / EN 1.4404 316L. Minimum PREN 24.0 per heat, verified on Mill MTC before fabrication. Standard 304, 304L, and standard 316 are not acceptable substitutes. No material substitution without written approval from the engineer of record. Heat-number traceability must be maintained through fabrication and installation.

For splash-zone or severe chloride service, the clause may need 2205 duplex or higher. Do not let "316L" carry every coastal condition.

## Add Finish and Weld Language

Coastal failures often begin at omissions:

- visible surfaces from mixed finish batches
- rough or contaminated surface
- weld heat tint left in place
- wrong fastener material
- uncleanable crevices

Add:

> Visible stainless shall be from controlled finish run, with grain direction and approved sample confirmed before fabrication. Weld heat tint shall be removed and passivated before exposure. Crevices, trapped water details, and carbon-steel contact shall be avoided or isolated.

That paragraph saves more projects than another generic grade table.

## What to Reject Before Fabrication

Reject or hold when:

- MTC cannot be matched to the pieces
- PREN is below the required floor
- Mo is missing or low for the grade
- finish batch does not match the approved sample
- substitution is proposed without written engineering approval
- weld tint or mixed-metal details are being treated as "site cleanup"

Once the wrong material is fabricated into panels, the claim becomes slower and more expensive.

## Coastal Spec Shortcut

A good coastal spec names the environment, the alloy, the chemistry floor, the document proof, the visible finish, and the substitution rule. If any one is missing, someone later may decide the cheaper option was "close enough."
