# A Customer Says the Stainless Rusted. What Should You Do First?

The first reply should not be "we will replace it." It should also not be "that is not our fault."

When a customer reports rust, the first job is to understand the mechanism. Stainless can stain from iron contamination, pit from chlorides, corrode under gaskets, crack near welds, or fail because the wrong grade was supplied. Those are different problems with different responsible parties.

Make the first response practical: preserve the evidence, identify the pattern, and keep the commercial answer open until the mechanism is clear.

## Do Not Clean the Evidence Away

Ask for these before anyone polishes, grinds, or passivates the part:

- close photos with a ruler
- wide photos showing where the part sits
- heat number or MTC
- grade and finish ordered
- installation date and first-rust date
- cleaning chemicals and frequency
- nearby welds, gaskets, clamps, carbon steel, or standing water

Corrosion evidence is fragile. Cleaning can remove the very pattern that tells you what happened.

## Read the Pattern Before Assigning Fault

| What the customer sees | Likely mechanism | First suspicion |
|---|---|---|
| Brown stain that wipes off | Iron contamination or tea staining | Handling, tools, cleaning, atmosphere |
| Small deep holes | Pitting | Chlorides, grade too low, rough surface |
| Rust under gasket, washer, clamp | Crevice corrosion | Design geometry plus chloride/moisture |
| Rust or cracks near weld HAZ | Sensitization, heat tint, weld issue | Welding route or post-weld treatment |
| Branching cracks in warm chloride service | Stress corrosion cracking | Stress + chloride + temperature |
| Grade chemistry does not match order | Substitution or identity failure | Supplier or trading chain |

That table is not the final verdict. It keeps the investigation from drifting.

## Check Grade Before Arguing Service

Run two parallel checks:

1. Does the material match the order?
2. Was the ordered grade suitable for the service?

If PMI says the material sold as 316L has no molybdenum, it is a supply issue. If PMI confirms 304 and the part was installed in a coastal splash zone, the material may match the order while the specification was still wrong.

For PMI limits, see [PMI testing on stainless steel](/knowledge-base/pmi-testing-stainless-steel). For pitting, see [what causes pitting corrosion](/knowledge-base/what-is-pitting-corrosion).

## What a Useful Complaint File Contains

Keep the file simple:

- PO, invoice, packing list, and MTC
- heat number photos on the material
- installed-location photos
- close defect photos with scale
- cleaning and service history
- PMI or lab chemistry results if grade is questioned
- weld-treatment record if the defect follows the weld
- conclusion: material, fabrication, design, maintenance, or mixed cause

Do not bury the conclusion in twenty pages of raw photos. A buyer or supplier should be able to see the chain of reasoning in one page.

## How to Answer the Customer

Use language that protects the relationship and the record:

> We have received the photos and are opening a corrosion review. Before we confirm liability or remedy, we need to identify the corrosion mechanism and match the affected part to the supplied heat. Please do not clean or repair the area until the requested photos, service details, and MTC/heat number are recorded.

That is not evasive. It is responsible.

## Corrosion Claim Shortcut

Corrosion complaints become expensive when the first answer is faster than the evidence. Identify the mechanism, check the grade, and separate supply fault from service condition before offering a remedy.

If the result becomes a formal supplier claim, use [how to file a stainless steel quality complaint](/knowledge-base/how-to-file-quality-complaint-stainless-steel). If the issue is coastal grade choice, use [is 304 enough for coastal use](/knowledge-base/is-304-enough-for-coastal-use).
