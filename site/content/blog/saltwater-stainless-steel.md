# What Stainless Steel Actually Survives Saltwater?

Saltwater is not one environment. A handrail that catches sea air twice a week, a bracket in the splash zone, and a heat-exchanger tube in warm seawater are three different corrosion problems. Treating all of them as "marine grade stainless" is how 316L gets blamed for jobs it was never meant to carry.

The first split is simple: salt in the air, saltwater on the surface, or saltwater around the part all the time. Once the part stays wet, especially in a warm climate or in a crevice, the alloy family has to move upward. Surface finish and passivation still matter, but they cannot turn a coastal grade into an immersion grade.

## What Does Saltwater Do First?

Seawater carries roughly 19,000 ppm chloride. Chloride attacks weak points in the passive film and starts [pitting corrosion](/knowledge-base/what-is-pitting-corrosion). The surface may still look mostly clean while a pit grows downward.

Crevices make the same environment much harsher. Under a gasket, inside a lap joint, below a bolt head, or under marine growth, oxygen disappears and chloride concentrates. A grade that survives on an open surface can fail in that trapped pocket.

Temperature tightens the margin again. Warm seawater in Southeast Asia, the Gulf Coast, or the Middle East sits well above the comfortable working margin for ordinary austenitic stainless in direct contact.

## Which Grade Fits Which Exposure?

| Exposure | What the steel sees | Sensible starting grade |
|---|---|---|
| Coastal air, washed by rain | Salt deposit dries and rinses off | 316L for exposed work; 304 only inland or protected |
| Marine air with poor cleaning | Salt remains on sheltered faces | 316L, with a smooth finish and maintenance plan |
| Splash zone or tidal wetting | Repeated seawater wetting and evaporation | 2205 duplex as the starting point |
| Continuous seawater immersion | Constant chloride, often fouling and crevices | 2205 for cooler service; 2507 or 254 SMO for warmer or critical duty |
| Hot seawater piping, desalination, offshore duty | High chloride, velocity, heat, consequence | 2507, 254 SMO, or qualified nickel alloy |

This table is a starting point, not a license to ignore geometry. A 316L flat panel in coastal air is a reasonable choice. A 316L bolt inside a wet crevice near the same coast may not be.

## Where 304 Stops Being a Good Bet

[304](/grades/304-stainless-steel) is a good indoor and fresh-water stainless. It is not a saltwater stainless. Near the sea, it can stain from airborne chloride alone, especially on brushed surfaces, hidden fixings, or places that are not washed by rain.

304 belongs only where the salt load is low and cleaning is believable: inland coastal buildings, protected interiors, or non-critical parts where staining can be accepted and cleaned. It should not be used for direct seawater contact, marine splash, or hidden coastal fasteners.

## Where 316L Helps, and Where It Runs Out

[316L](/grades/316l-stainless-steel) earns its reputation because molybdenum improves chloride pitting resistance. For exposed coastal architecture, food equipment near the coast, fish-processing interiors, and parts that see salt air rather than standing seawater, it is often the practical baseline.

But 316L is not an immersion grade by default. Warm seawater, stagnant water, biofouling, gasket pockets, and welded crevices can push it past its limit. The common mistake is to upgrade from 304 to 316L and then stop thinking. In true saltwater service, 316L may only be the first step up the ladder.

## When Duplex Becomes the Safer Answer

[2205 duplex](/grades/2205-duplex-stainless-steel) is the practical starting point once the part is repeatedly wet with seawater or cannot drain cleanly. It gives a higher PREN than 316L and has much better resistance to chloride stress corrosion cracking.

For warm immersion, desalination brine, high-consequence offshore parts, or seawater systems with crevices and fouling, the discussion moves to 2507 super duplex or 254 SMO. The higher price is usually easier to defend than replacing pitted parts after one season.

## What to Check Before You Choose

- Is the part only near salt air, or will seawater actually sit on it?
- Will the surface dry and rinse, or will salt stay in a hidden pocket?
- What is the highest surface temperature, not the average air temperature alone?
- Are fasteners, welds, gaskets, and brackets the same alloy logic as the visible panel?
- Can maintenance realistically remove salt before it concentrates?

If those answers are vague, do not use the cheapest familiar grade as the default. Saltwater punishes optimistic specifications.

## The Useful Shortcut

Use 316L for exposed coastal atmosphere when the surface can be cleaned. Use 2205 when seawater touches the part repeatedly. Use 2507 or 254 SMO when immersion is warm, creviced, or expensive to repair.

That is the line most failed jobs cross: they treat saltwater as a stronger version of coastal air. It is not. It is a different environment, and the grade has to move with it.
