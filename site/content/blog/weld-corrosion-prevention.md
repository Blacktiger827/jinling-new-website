# Why Do Stainless Steel Welds Corrode First?

Stainless welds corrode first because welding changes both the surface and the heat-affected zone. The parent sheet may still have a clean passive film, while the weld zone has heat tint, chromium depletion, residual stress, possible root oxidation, and sometimes the wrong filler.

That is why a weld can look acceptable at dispatch and still become the first rust line in service.

---

## What Welding Damages

| Weld-zone issue | What it means | Prevention |
|---|---|---|
| Heat tint | Oxide color with chromium depletion beneath | Control purge and remove tint before release |
| Sensitization | Chromium carbides at grain boundaries | Use L-grades or stabilized grades; control heat |
| Root oxidation / sugaring | Oxidized root bead on the inside surface | Back purge tube and pipe welds |
| Iron contamination | Carbon-steel grinding dust or tooling transfer | Use dedicated stainless tools |
| Residual stress | SCC risk in chloride service | Control heat input and post-weld treatment route |

The visible color is only part of the story. A polished weld can still be vulnerable if the HAZ was sensitized or the root side was oxidized.

---

## Heat Tint Is a Corrosion Warning

Heat tint is more than discoloration. Chromium moves into the oxide layer, leaving the metal below it poorer in chromium. That depleted layer is easier for chloride, acid, or cleaning chemistry to attack.

| Weld color | Release reading |
|---|---|
| Bright to light straw | Usually acceptable after cleaning/passivation, depending on service |
| Gold / brown | Needs cleaning and passivation; check service severity |
| Blue / purple | Must be pickled or mechanically removed |
| Gray / black | Reject or rework; purge or heat control failed |

Passivation alone does not remove a heavy heat-tint layer. It can form a passive film on a surface that is still chromium-depleted underneath. In corrosion service, that is not enough.

---

## Sensitization: The Hidden Problem

Sensitization happens when standard-carbon austenitic stainless spends time in the 425–815 °C range. Carbon combines with chromium at grain boundaries, leaving nearby metal less able to form a stable passive film.

Prevention is usually straightforward:

- Use 304L or 316L for welded work.
- Match low-carbon filler to low-carbon base metal.
- Keep heat input and interpass temperature under control.
- Use stabilized grades such as 321 or 347 for repeated high-temperature service.

If the service is nitric, hot chloride, or other high-consequence corrosion duty, sensitization is not a theoretical issue. It is a release issue.

---

## Back Purge and Root Quality

Tube and pipe welds often fail from the inside first. The outside bead looked clean; the root bead oxidized because the purge was weak or lost too early.

Back purge is critical for:

- sanitary tube
- food and pharma pipework
- chloride service
- closed sections that cannot be cleaned after welding
- any internal surface that becomes the process-contact side

For these welds, oxygen control during the root pass matters more than how good the outside bead looks in a photo.

---

## Filler and Post-Weld Treatment

The filler should protect the service class of the joint.

| Base or joint | Usual filler direction |
|---|---|
| 304L | ER308L |
| 316L | ER316L |
| 304 to 316L | Usually ER316L |
| Stainless to carbon steel | ER309L |
| 2205 duplex | ER2209 |

After welding, remove oxide and contamination before passivation. For light weld color, cleaning plus passivation may be enough. For blue, purple, gray, or black tint, pickling or mechanical removal comes first.

---

## Weld-Corrosion Prevention Checklist

Before releasing welded stainless:

- Confirm base grade and filler grade.
- Confirm L-grade where welded corrosion service matters.
- Confirm purge requirement for root-side service surfaces.
- Classify heat tint before cleaning.
- Remove visible oxide to the required level.
- Passivate after oxide removal.
- Keep carbon-steel tools away from stainless finishing.
- Record treatment method where the order is corrosion-critical.

This checklist is deliberately simple. Most weld-corrosion failures come from skipping one ordinary step, not from an exotic metallurgy problem.

## FAQ

**Can passivation fix blue heat tint?** No. Blue or darker tint needs pickling or mechanical removal before passivation.

**Why use L-grade filler?** It keeps carbon low in the weld metal and reduces sensitization risk around the joint.

**Does every weld need back purge?** Not every open plate weld. Tube, pipe, sanitary, and closed-section service surfaces usually do.

**Can I weld 304 to 316L?** Yes. Use a filler that protects the more demanding side of the joint, commonly ER316L for corrosion service.

**Why do welds rust when the base sheet does not?** The weld zone has heat tint, chromium depletion, residual stress, and possible contamination. The base metal may never have seen those conditions.
