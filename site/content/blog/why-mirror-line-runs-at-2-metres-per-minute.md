# Why Our 8K Mirror Stainless Steel Line Runs at 2 Metres a Minute

8K mirror stainless that hazes up two months after installation is usually exposing a line-speed problem. Running the polishing line at 2 m/min instead of 30 gives oscillating buffing heads time to erase directional grit. Low-sulfur base steel removes one common pinhole source. A final raking-light inspection keeps a weak sheet from becoming the buyer's problem.

A mirror stainless panel leaves the [polishing line](/solutions/capabilities/surface-finish-capability) looking flawless. The sample goes to the client. The client approves. The container arrives. The panel goes up in a hotel lobby.

Two months later, someone turns off the main lights and leaves the raking downlights on. The reflection that looked clean in warehouse fluorescents suddenly reads milky from across the room. The client calls. Nothing has technically broken. Nothing can be technically claimed. But the mirror has aged badly in a way the sample never hinted at.

Almost every time this happens, the root cause is polishing-line speed.

## What Polishing Speed Actually Does to a Mirror Surface

No.8 mirror polishing is a stack of progressively finer abrasive passes. Each pass removes the scratches left by the last one. The final pass is supposed to leave a surface whose peaks and valleys are small enough that reflected light stays coherent.

At high line speed, two things go wrong:

1. **Each buffing head spends less time on each square inch.** The belt or pad cannot fully erase the directional marks from the previous grit. They survive as faint, parallel lines: invisible in flat light, unmistakable under a raking beam.
2. **Heat accumulates unevenly across the sheet.** Local temperature spikes push the sheet through small deformations. You can see this in a shallow, wavy reflection when the panel is tilted. The polish itself is fine; the flatness is not.

Running slower is the counter-intuitive fix. When the buffing head has time to wipe every path from every angle, the surface loses its memory of the earlier grit pattern. The result is more than a brighter sheet. The surface structure underneath the shine is cleaner, and the reflection stays flatter because heat has more time to dissipate evenly.

Our mirror line runs at 2 m/min against an industry norm of about 30. Twenty-four circular oscillating buffing heads rotate 360° on every pass to eliminate directional marks. The cost is throughput; the return is a uniform, distortion-free reflection across the full sheet.

## 2 m/min vs 30 m/min: What Actually Changes

| Outcome you can measure | Line at ~30 m/min | Line at ~2 m/min |
|---|---|---|
| Surface roughness, Ra | Typically 0.04–0.06 µm | Consistently ≤ 0.03 µm |
| 60° gloss (GU) | 400–500 | > 900 |
| Directional grit visibility | Visible under raking light | Not visible; cross-polished |
| Haze under raking downlight | Sheet-to-sheet variation, noticeable | Uniform across lot |
| Heat-related waviness | Present on thinner gauges | Minimised by slower dwell |
| Yield to [ASTM A480](https://www.astm.org/a0480_a0480m-22b.html) No.8 | Batch-dependent | Consistent |
| Throughput per shift | High | Low, the real trade-off |

The trade-off is real: we produce less mirror per shift. That is the whole bargain. Buyers who need mirror tonnage at commodity price points should buy from a 30 m/min line. Buyers whose panels are going into a lobby, an elevator, a feature wall, or a brand-driven retail fitout are paying for the difference.

## The Oscillating Head Matters as Much as the Speed

A mirror line at 2 m/min with straight-path heads will still leave directional grit, only finer. The second half of the decision is head geometry.

Our line uses twenty-four buffing heads arranged to oscillate in a small circular pattern rather than a straight stroke. Each head wipes the surface from every angle as the sheet passes underneath. Grit lines from each pass cross themselves and each other often enough that the surface ends up without a dominant direction.

That is why our mirror reads the same head-on, at 45°, or at grazing angle. A directional mirror brightens and dims as you walk past it. A properly cross-polished mirror stays the same.

## The Sulfur Problem That Polishing Speed Cannot Fix

Fast or slow, there is one defect line speed cannot prevent: sulfide-inclusion pinholes.

Steel is never chemically perfect. Sulfur is a normal residual element, usually under 0.030% in standard [304 per ASTM A240](https://www.astm.org/a0240_a0240m-23.html). During hot rolling, sulfur bonds with manganese into elongated inclusions (MnS). When those inclusions reach the surface and get polished, they pull out. What they leave behind is a pinhole, a microscopic dark dot that reads as a flaw under any mirror inspection.

The fix is not on the polishing line. It is at the purchase order for the base coil. Quality mirror finish starts with quality base material. We source low-sulfur coil (S ≤ 0.005%) for the mirror line specifically to avoid this failure mode.

The standard 304 ceiling is 0.030% sulfur. Our mirror-line base coils are held under 0.005%, which is six times tighter. That one sourcing decision removes the entire pinhole failure mode before any buffing head touches the sheet.

## Sulfur Content and Mirror Pinhole Risk

| Base coil sulfur content | Where you usually find it | Polished mirror outcome |
|---|---|---|
| ≤ 0.030% (A240 standard ceiling) | Commodity 304 coil | Visible pinholes on 8K under raking light |
| ≤ 0.015% (low-sulfur spec) | Mid-tier mill supply | Pinholes reduced but still present on large panels |
| ≤ 0.005% (our mirror base) | Specifically sourced low-S coil | Pinhole-free at No.8 / Super 8K release |

You cannot test for this by looking at a sample. It has to be an upstream sourcing decision, and you will only see it on the mill test certificate.

## The Haze Wall

Even with slow speed, cross-polished heads, and low-sulfur base, mirror sheets occasionally fail. Haze can appear from a film handling mistake, a bad belt, or a moment of airborne dust during a batch. The question is whether the sheet leaves the factory anyway.

On our line, the last station is a raking-light inspection booth. A trained inspector walks each sheet under a focused light and looks for haze, pinholes, polish lines, film marks, and anything the instruments might miss. We also run laser-cutting tests on representative samples before mass production, especially on harder-to-polish grades like 201. If a sheet crosses the internal haze wall, it does not leave our floor.

That wall is not "clean" or "acceptable" or "within tolerance." It is a release habit we actually enforce. If a sheet crosses it, the sheet is pulled. The rule matters less for any individual sheet than for the lot consistency it forces on the whole line: every operator knows where the wall is, and production habits organise around it.

## Three Questions to Ask a Mirror Supplier

You do not need to visit four factories. You need three answers:

1. **What is your polishing line speed on No.8 mirror?** If the answer is above about 10 m/min, the supplier is optimising for volume, not for reflection quality. Fine for some jobs, not for a lobby.
2. **Are your buffing heads linear or oscillating?** Straight-path produces directional mirror. Oscillating produces omnidirectional mirror. The difference reads on installed panels, not on a sample.
3. **What is the sulfur spec on your mirror base coil?** Below 0.01% means the supplier understands the pinhole failure mode. "Whatever is standard" means they do not.

The supplier who can answer all three clearly is running a mirror line, not a polishing line that happens to produce mirror.

## FAQ

**Is 2 m/min universal for No.8 mirror, or specific to Jinling?** It is specific. Most Chinese and global polishing lines run 20-40 m/min. A few boutique mirror producers run slower. The speed is not in any standard; the outcomes (Ra, gloss, haze) are what standards like [ASTM A480](https://www.astm.org/a0480_a0480m-22b.html) actually govern.

**Does slow-polished mirror cost more?** Yes, in production cost per square metre. It also has a dramatically lower rejection rate and a longer service life in visible installations, which usually shifts the total-cost math. Ask for the per-sheet rejection rate if you want to see the offset.

**Can I tell from a sample if the polishing line was fast or slow?** Not reliably from one sample. You can sometimes see directional grit under a raking light if the line was straight-path; you usually cannot detect line speed otherwise. The test is lot consistency across a container, which you only see after delivery.

**Does low-sulfur base matter if I am buying 430 mirror?** Yes, arguably more. 430 has less corrosion reserve and fewer tolerances for surface defects. We apply the same low-sulfur sourcing logic to 430 mirror as to 304.

**Is this different from Super 8K or No.10 / No.12?** The process described here is the foundation for No.8 mirror. Super 8K (Ra < 0.03 µm) and the No.10 / No.12 tiers apply additional passes on top of this base; the speed and head-geometry decisions are the same starting point.
