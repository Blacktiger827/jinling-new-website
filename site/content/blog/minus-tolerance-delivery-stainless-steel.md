# Why Did the Stainless Arrive Thinner Than Ordered?

A buyer orders 1.5 mm stainless sheet. Receiving inspection reads 1.44 mm. The first reaction is usually frustration: "They shipped thin material."

Sometimes that is true. Often it is not. Most stainless standards allow material to arrive below nominal thickness. If the purchase order only says "1.5 mm per ASTM A480" or a similar default standard, a below-nominal reading can still be fully conforming.

That is the uncomfortable part of minus tolerance: the micrometer may be telling the truth, and the supplier may still be inside the contract.

## Nominal Thickness Is Not a Guaranteed Minimum

Rolling stainless is not machining one exact dimension. The mill controls a moving strip or plate inside an allowed band. Standards turn that production reality into thickness tolerance.

For many cold-rolled sheet orders, the allowed band is bilateral: a little above nominal, a little below nominal. Hot-rolled plate often uses a fixed negative allowance with a wider positive side. Either way, nominal is the name of the size, not always the lowest acceptable size.

| What the PO says | What the mill may legally ship |
|---|---|
| "1.0 mm per standard tolerance" | A sheet below 1.0 mm, if still inside the tolerance table |
| "1.5 mm nominal, actual weight billing" | A thinner sheet may arrive, but the invoice follows the scale weight |
| "1.5 mm minimum actual" | Any reading below 1.5 mm becomes a non-conformance |
| "1.5 mm +0.07 / -0 mm" | The mill must target above nominal and exclude minus tolerance |

This is why a thickness claim starts with the PO. The standard default is not a moral argument. It is the rule both sides bought unless the order narrowed it.

## When Minus Tolerance Is Harmless

Standard minus tolerance is usually acceptable for general sheet metal work, decorative panels with margin, stock that will be cut and formed later, and applications where the part design does not depend on minimum section thickness.

In those cases, paying for zero-minus can be unnecessary. The extra cost buys a tighter mill target, but it may not buy a better finished part.

The question is practical: if this coil arrives at the lowest legal thickness, will anything fail?

If the answer is no, standard tolerance is fine.

## When It Becomes a Real Problem

Minus tolerance becomes expensive when the drawing quietly treats nominal as minimum. That happens in pressure shells, structural brackets, food-equipment panels that must stay stiff, precision assemblies with fixed gaps, and any part where rework after fabrication is worse than paying for better input material.

For pressure or structural work, the calculation should use minimum guaranteed thickness. If the design needs 5.0 mm minimum and the standard permits -0.30 mm, then 5.0 mm nominal is not enough. Either order thicker material or write a no-minus requirement.

## The PO Clause That Prevents the Fight

Use one of these clauses, depending on the risk:

> "Thickness 1.5 mm nominal, standard tolerance per ASTM A480."

Use this only when standard minus is acceptable.

> "Thickness 1.5 mm +0.07 / -0 mm. No measured point below 1.5 mm."

Use this when nominal must be the floor.

> "Weight basis: actual scale weight. Certified weight ticket required for each coil or bundle."

Use this when the commercial issue is paying theoretical weight for material that may run thin.

For critical work, combine them:

> "1.5 mm +0.07 / -0 mm, per ASTM A480 except minus tolerance excluded. Weight basis: actual scale weight. MTC or release report to include measured thickness."

## How to Check the Delivery

Measure like you may need to defend the result. Use a calibrated micrometer, take readings across the width, avoid the sheared edge zone, and record the coil or bundle number. Compare the readings with the PO first, then the cited standard.

If the PO did not exclude minus tolerance, a below-nominal reading may be disappointing but not rejectable. If the PO did exclude it, even one confirmed reading below the minimum should put the lot on hold.
