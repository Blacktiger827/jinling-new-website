# Architectural Stainless Steel: How to Specify Grade, Finish, and Detail by Exposure Zone

Architectural stainless steel is not chosen by render image. It is chosen by exposure.

A bright lobby panel, a coastal soffit, and an airport wall lining may all start as "stainless sheet" in the drawing set. They should not be specified the same way. The cleaner order is exposure zone first, then grade, then finish, then detailing and maintenance access.

When facade stainless fails, the problem is rarely one dramatic mistake. It is usually a quiet stack of small misses: 304 used near salt air, horizontal ledges that hold dirt, mixed grain direction, aggressive cleaning, or a mirror finish placed where daily contact will make every scratch visible.

## Start With the Exposure Zone

Grade selection should follow the environment, not the visual target.

| Exposure | Practical baseline | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Interior dry lobby, lift cab, retail wall | 304 or 430 | Finish and scratch visibility matter more than corrosion margin. |
| Covered exterior, low pollution | 304 | Use drainage, avoid dirt traps, and plan periodic cleaning. |
| Urban exterior or industrial air | 316L | Pollution films can hold chlorides and sulfur compounds against the surface. |
| Coastal exterior, splash risk, tunnel portals | 316L review; duplex for severe zones | Avoid rough finishes, open joints, and uncleanable ledges. |
| Swimming pool ceiling or warm chloride vapor | Specialist review | 316L may not be enough where chloride vapor concentrates above water. |

The common error is treating "exterior" as one category. A shaded building 20 km inland and a brushed column beside a seaside road do not age the same way.

For coastal grade logic, compare this article with [is 304 enough for coastal use](/knowledge-base/is-304-enough-for-coastal-use).

## Choose the Finish for Service, Not the Sample Board Alone

On a sample table, mirror, hairline, No.4, and bead-blasted panels all look controlled. On a building, the finish has to survive light, hands, cleaning tools, and weather.

| Finish | Where it works well | Where it becomes risky |
|---|---|---|
| No.4 / Hairline | Elevators, columns, lobby walls, appliances | Grain mismatch is obvious if panels rotate or come from different batches. |
| 8K mirror | Feature walls, luxury interiors, controlled-touch areas | Shows handling scratches, distortion, and cleaning marks quickly. |
| 2B / BA | Back panels, formed parts, low-touch surfaces | Can look too plain for visible architectural faces unless intentionally designed. |
| AFP over brushed or mirror | Fingerprint-heavy interiors | Coating consistency and repair method must be confirmed before approval. |
| Bead blast / matte | Soft glare control | Rougher surface can hold dirt and raise tea-staining risk outdoors. |

For architectural work, the finish line in the purchase order should not stop at "hairline." Add grain direction, protective film, panel orientation, and whether replacement panels must match the original batch.

## Detail the Building So the Steel Can Stay Clean

Stainless steel survives by keeping a passive surface. Design details decide whether that surface can actually stay clean.

Four details matter most:

- **Drainage:** horizontal ledges, open seams, and underside returns collect chlorides and soot.
- **Grain direction:** vertical grain sheds water better and hides handling marks more gracefully than horizontal grain.
- **Fasteners:** carbon-steel contact and unsealed crevices create local corrosion points even when the panel grade is correct.
- **Cleaning access:** if maintenance crews cannot reach it safely, the surface will not be cleaned on schedule.

Tea staining on exterior stainless is usually not deep structural corrosion. It is a brown surface stain caused by salt, dirt, and moisture sitting on the passive film. It still damages the building story because the owner sees "rust" on a premium material.

## Write the Specification in One Line

A useful architectural stainless line sounds more like this:

> 316L stainless sheet, No.4 vertical grain, Ra target agreed on control sample, PVC film both sides, panel grain direction marked, MTC supplied by heat number, exterior cleaning access confirmed.

That line is stronger than "316 stainless brushed finish" because it tells the supplier and fabricator what has to be controlled. For visual work, the control sample should also define acceptable color tone, gloss range, grain density, and corner protection.

## When 304 Is Still the Right Choice

Not every architectural job needs 316L. For interior walls, lift cabins, retail displays, and dry commercial fit-out, 304 is often the balanced choice. In controlled indoor areas, the bigger risk is not corrosion. It is inconsistent finish, poor edge protection, dents, or panels installed with grain direction reversed.

430 can also be valid for indoor decorative panels where corrosion load is low and cost discipline matters. It is magnetic and less formable than 304, but it can carry mirror or brushed finishes well when the application is honest about exposure.

The wrong move is not choosing 304. The wrong move is choosing 304 for a salt, humidity, or cleaning-chemical environment and hoping polish will compensate for alloy margin.

## Architectural Release Checklist

Before approving the order, confirm these points together:

- exposure zone and cleaning schedule
- grade and standard, including whether 316L or duplex is required
- visible-face finish, grain direction, and control sample
- panel orientation, edge return, fastener isolation, and drainage
- protective film, packaging, and handling route to site
- MTC, batch traceability, and replacement-panel matching rules

Architectural stainless works best when the material, detail, and maintenance story are designed as one system. The building does not care which department forgot a line on the drawing. It only shows the result.
