# What Actually Controls Stainless Steel Lead Time?

Lead time is not one number. A supplier can have the steel in stock and still miss the shipping window because slitting, polishing, inspection, export documents, or container booking was not ready.

The better question is not "what is your lead time?" It is "which route is this order taking?"

## The Route Decides the Clock

| Order route | What usually controls timing | What to confirm early |
|---|---|---|
| repeat stock order | picking, packing, booking | same spec, same documents, same packing |
| first trial order | sample approval and PO clarity | grade, finish, tolerance, packaging |
| processed sheet or coil | slitting, CTL, polishing, film | line slot and finish release |
| mill-order grade or size | production schedule | heat availability and minimum run |
| project or coded order | inspection and documents | MTC, certificate type, third-party hold |
| export shipment | booking and customs file | COO, export licence if applicable, loading photos |

Most delays are not mysterious. They happen where the order crosses from one team to another: sales to production, production to inspection, inspection to documents, documents to freight.

## Factory Ready Is Not Ship Ready

One common misunderstanding is the gap between material being finished at the factory and the shipment being ready to leave the port. Factory ready means production and packing are done. Ship ready means the document pack, booking, loading plan, and export clearance path are also lined up.

That gap may be small on repeat orders. It can become the real lead-time issue when the buyer needs a special certificate, a third-party inspection release, a destination-specific origin document, or export licence confirmation for a covered product.

## What Makes an Order Slow

Non-stock grades, unusual thickness, tight tolerance, mirror or AFP surface, third-party inspection, special certificate wording, destination-specific origin documents, and mixed-container packing can all add time.

None of these are bad by themselves. They only become a problem when they are discovered after the deposit is paid or after the customer has promised a date downstream.

## What to Ask Before the PO

Ask the supplier to break the schedule into stages:

- material availability
- processing slot
- inspection or sample approval point
- document preparation
- packing and loading
- export clearance and vessel booking

That gives both sides a schedule that can be managed. A single date does not.

## PO Wording

Use wording like:

> "Supplier to confirm lead time by route: stock or mill order, processing steps, inspection hold points, document pack, packing date, and vessel booking target."

For first orders, connect the lead-time discussion to the document pack. A fast shipment with the wrong MTC or origin document is not actually fast.
