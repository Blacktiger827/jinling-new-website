# When Is Weld Color a Corrosion Problem?

Weld color on stainless is a corrosion clue. Straw, gold, purple, blue, and grey all point to oxide thickness and possible chromium depletion underneath. The darker the color, the less comfortable the surface is in chloride, food, pharma, marine, or visible architectural service.

The trick is not to panic at every tint. It is to know when color can be passivated, when it must be pickled, and when the weld route itself needs fixing.

## Read the Color Ladder

| Weld color | What it usually means | Typical release decision |
|---|---|---|
| Bright silver | Good purge and heat control | Clean, passivate if required |
| Light straw | Light oxide | Often acceptable in mild service; passivate |
| Gold or bronze | More oxide, some risk | Clean or light pickle if corrosion service |
| Purple or blue | Chromium-depleted layer likely | Pickle or electrochemical clean, then passivate |
| Grey or black | Heavy oxidation or purge failure | Remove, reassess, possibly repair weld |

For sanitary, pharmaceutical, marine, and chemical service, the acceptable color band is much narrower. A weld that passes on a dry indoor bracket may fail immediately on a food tube or coastal tank.

## Heat Tint Versus Oxide Scale

Heat tint is a thin colored oxide formed around welds and local heating. Oxide scale is heavier, duller, and usually comes from sustained high temperature such as hot rolling, furnace exposure, or severe purge failure.

Heat tint can often be removed with electrochemical cleaning or pickling. Heavy scale may need mechanical removal first because acid alone cannot attack it evenly.

## Why the Color Hurts Corrosion Resistance

The visible oxide contains chromium pulled from the metal just below the surface. If enough chromium is depleted, the passive film cannot rebuild with the same strength. In chloride service, this becomes a pit-starting zone.

Passivation alone does not remove a blue or purple oxide. It may improve the surface it can reach, but the weak layer remains. That is why darker tint needs removal before passivation.

## Match the Cleanup to the Service

| Service | Treatment attitude |
|---|---|
| Dry indoor structural work | Light straw may be accepted if documented |
| General outdoor or mild chemical | Remove gold and darker tint |
| Food, dairy, pharma | Very low tint tolerance; root condition matters |
| Marine or chloride service | Remove visible tint before release |
| Mirror or decorative finish | Visual release may be stricter than corrosion release |

The finish also changes the choice. Mechanical polishing may remove tint but can ruin a mirror or brushed match. Electrochemical cleaning may preserve appearance better on visible welds.

## Prevention Is Cheaper Than Cleanup

Heat tint usually points back to shielding, purge, travel speed, heat input, interpass temperature, or poor gas coverage. If the same weld keeps turning blue, the cleaning step is downstream of the real problem. Fix the welding route.

For tube and pipe roots, purge oxygen is the whole game. Once the inside sugars or turns grey, post-weld access may be too limited to recover the surface properly.

## The Release Rule

Light tint in mild service may be manageable. Blue, purple, grey, or black tint in corrosion-sensitive service is a release problem, not a cosmetic preference.
