# Why Do Stainless Welds Sometimes Corrode Along the Grain Boundaries?

When stainless corrodes in a line beside a weld, the problem may not be the open surface. It may be the heat history.

Intergranular corrosion happens when chromium is pulled out of the grain-boundary area, usually by chromium carbide formation during welding or slow cooling. The surface may still look like stainless, but the grain boundary no longer has enough chromium to hold the passive film.

This is called sensitization.

## Where Sensitization Comes From

The risky temperature range is roughly 425-815°C. Welding heat-affected zones pass through that range. If carbon is high enough and the heat cycle is long enough, chromium carbides form at the grain boundaries.

The practical result:

- chromium near the boundary drops
- the boundary loses corrosion resistance
- attack follows the grain boundary rather than the open surface
- leaks may appear near welds while the base plate still looks healthy

Passivation cannot reverse this. The problem is metallurgical, not surface dirt.

## How to Prevent It

| Situation | Better choice |
|---|---|
| Normal welded 304 or 316 equipment | Use 304L or 316L. |
| High-temperature service | Consider stabilized grades such as 321 or 347. |
| Heavy fabrication with high heat input | Control weld procedure and interpass temperature. |
| Suspected sensitization | Test before assuming surface treatment will fix it. |

L-grades limit carbon to reduce carbide formation during welding. That is why the "L" on 304L and 316L matters most when the part will be welded.

For more on L-grade meaning, see [what does L mean in stainless steel](/knowledge-base/what-does-l-mean-stainless-steel).

## What to Check in a Failure

If corrosion follows welds or grain boundaries, collect:

- MTC carbon value
- whether the material was 304/316 or 304L/316L
- weld procedure and repair-weld history
- post-weld treatment record
- photos showing the attack path
- metallographic test if the claim is serious

ASTM A262 testing is commonly used to evaluate susceptibility to intergranular corrosion. A lab report matters because surface photos alone can confuse IGC with pitting, crevice corrosion, or heat-tint attack.

## Responsibility Boundary

If the supplier delivered standard 304 when the PO required 304L, that is a supply issue. If the buyer ordered standard 304 and then welded it into a corrosive service, the material may match the order while the specification was wrong. If the correct L-grade was delivered but welding was uncontrolled, the responsibility may sit with fabrication.

This is why the MTC and weld record need to be read together.

## IGC Shortcut

Intergranular corrosion is usually built into the part before service starts. Prevent it with the right low-carbon or stabilized grade, a controlled weld route, and the right test where the service is sensitive.
