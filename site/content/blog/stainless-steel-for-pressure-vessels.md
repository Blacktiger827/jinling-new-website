# Why Can the Right Stainless Grade Still Fail Vessel Release?

A pressure-vessel order can specify 316L and still fail receiving inspection. The grade may be correct. The problem is often the route: wrong product-form standard, missing ASME SA wording, unclear heat treatment, or a certificate that the Authorized Inspector cannot accept.

For vessel work, stainless selection is a release chain as much as a metallurgy choice.

## Grade Is Only the First Gate

304L and 316L cover many moderate pressure-vessel duties. They weld well and have good corrosion resistance for a broad range of water, food, chemical, and process environments. But the grade name does not tell the whole code story.

The vessel team still has to check four things before ordering:

- service chemistry and chloride level
- design temperature and allowable stress route
- product form, such as plate, pipe, forging, or fitting
- certificate type, heat treatment, and traceability wording

If any one of these is wrong, the material can become unusable for code fabrication even when the chemistry looks acceptable.

## Product Form Decides the SA Specification

Do not order every component under the same stainless description. Plate, pipe, flanges, and fittings each need their own route.

| Component | Typical ASME route | What to check on the certificate |
|---|---|---|
| Plate, sheet, strip | SA-240 | grade, UNS, heat number, solution annealed condition |
| Pipe | SA-312 or SA-358 | seamless/welded route, NDE requirements, heat traceability |
| Forgings and flanges | SA-182 | forged condition, class, grade prefix such as F316L |
| Fittings | SA-403 | WP grade, heat treatment, forming route |

ASTM and ASME specifications can share chemistry. That does not mean an ASTM-only certificate will always clear ASME release. Many inspectors want the ASME SA designation shown explicitly, because that is what the quality plan and code package reference.

The safest PO wording is plain:

> "Material to be certified to ASTM A240 / ASME SA-240, dual-certified, EN 10204 Type 3.1 MTC, solution annealed condition stated."

Adjust the specification for pipe, flange, or fitting orders.

## Temperature Can Change the Grade Answer

Low-carbon grades are attractive because they reduce sensitization risk after welding. That does not make them the default for every temperature.

For elevated-temperature service, confirm the current ASME allowable-stress table before using 304L or 316L. Stabilized or high-carbon variants such as 321, 347, or 304H may be the cleaner code route, depending on temperature and design life. For chloride service, 316L may still be too lean if temperature, concentration, and stress combine. Duplex or higher alloy material may be needed.

For cryogenic service, austenitic stainless is usually suitable because it keeps toughness well, but the MTC and project specification still decide whether Charpy evidence is required.

The habit is simple: do not choose the grade from a corrosion table alone. Read it together with temperature, pressure, weld route, and the certificate package.

## What the MTC Must Prove

For pressure-vessel material, the MTC is not paperwork after the real work. It is part of the real work.

A useful certificate should show:

- producing mill and heat number
- exact grade and UNS number
- ASME SA specification, with the trade name treated as a helper rather than the release basis
- heat-specific chemical and mechanical results
- heat treatment condition, usually solution annealed for austenitic stainless
- certificate type required by the project, commonly an EN 10204 Type 3.1 style mill certificate when that document system is used, with 3.2 if the owner or sector requires third-party witness

If the certificate is weak, the shop may not be able to repair the problem locally. Retesting can help some gaps. Missing original certification or wrong specification route can delay the vessel by weeks.

## What Must Clear Before Cutting?

The best time to catch a vessel-material problem is before the plate is cut. Once material enters fabrication, every documentation weakness becomes harder to solve.

Use this release order:

1. Confirm the product-form SA specification.
2. Confirm grade, UNS, and any temperature-driven variant.
3. Confirm the heat treatment wording.
4. Confirm certificate type and traceability.
5. Confirm the inspector or owner accepts the document language.

That order keeps "316L" from becoming a false sense of safety. The right alloy still needs the right code route.
