# How to Weld Stainless Steel: When to Use TIG, MIG, Stick, or SAW

Stainless welding is not carbon-steel welding with a shinier base metal. Stainless expands more, conducts heat less, and can lose corrosion resistance at the weld if heat input, filler, purge, or post-weld cleaning is treated casually.

Most good stainless welds come down to five controls: choose the right process, match the filler, control heat, protect both sides of the weld, and remove heat tint before the part enters service.

---

## Which Welding Process Fits the Job?

Process choice starts with wall thickness, access, surface cleanliness, and production speed. There is no single "best" process.

| Process | Where it fits | Watch point |
|---|---|---|
| TIG / GTAW | Thin sheet, sanitary tube, visible welds, food/pharma work | Slow, but gives the best purge and bead control |
| MIG / GMAW | Production work, thicker sections, general fabrication | Faster, but heat input and gas mix need discipline |
| Stick / SMAW | Field repair, site work, access-limited jobs | More cleanup and operator variation |
| SAW | Thick plate and long straight seams | High deposition, usually flat/horizontal only |

For [sanitary tube](/products/stainless-steel-sanitary-tube), TIG is usually the serious answer because internal cleanliness matters. For thick industrial assemblies, MIG or SAW may be more realistic once the WPS controls heat input and cleaning.

---

## Filler Choice Is a Release Decision

The filler is not a small consumable detail. It decides carbon level, dilution behavior, ferrite balance, and whether the weld still belongs to the same service class as the base metal.

| Base metal or joint | Normal filler route | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| 304 / 304L | ER308L / E308L | Low-carbon filler is safer for welded corrosion service |
| 316 / 316L | ER316L / E316L | Keeps the Mo-bearing weld route aligned with 316L service |
| 321 | ER347 / E347 | Niobium-stabilized filler is used because Ti does not transfer cleanly |
| Duplex 2205 | ER2209 / E2209 | Over-alloyed to help restore phase balance |
| Stainless to carbon steel | ER309L / E309L | Handles dilution from the carbon-steel side |

Do not use carbon-steel filler on stainless. Do not use 308L for a 316L joint just because it is already on the shelf. And if the job is duplex, treat filler, heat input, interpass temperature, and ferrite range as release items, not preferences.

---

## Heat Input and Interpass Control

Heat does two kinds of damage. Too much total heat can distort the assembly and keep the heat-affected zone in a sensitizing range. Too little control between passes can damage duplex phase balance or leave the welder chasing fit-up with extra passes.

For austenitic 304/316 work, keep heat input low enough to control distortion and limit time in the sensitization window. For duplex, interpass temperature is stricter, often capped around 100 °C, because phase balance and sigma-phase risk are more sensitive.

The practical shop checks are simple:

- Use a written WPS, not inherited carbon-steel settings.
- Record interpass temperature on duplex and critical austenitic work.
- Avoid unnecessary weave width and extra passes.
- Use tack sequence, fixturing, and balanced welding to control distortion.
- Clean oxide before release, not after the customer complains.

---

## Shielding, Back Purge, and Internal Surface

The outside bead can look good while the root side is already a corrosion site. This is common on tube, pipe, and closed sections when back purge is skipped or lost before the root is complete.

For TIG stainless, argon shielding and proper back purge are the normal route on tube and pipe. For MIG stainless, use a stainless-appropriate gas mix, not a carbon-steel CO₂-heavy blend. Excess oxidation at the pool surface steals chromium from the part of the weld that needs it most.

On food, beverage, pharmaceutical, and clean process lines, purge quality is not optional. The inside of the joint is the product-contact surface.

---

## Heat Tint: What Must Be Removed?

Heat tint is a corrosion warning, not a color problem alone. It marks an oxidized and chromium-depleted surface. The darker the tint, the less comfortable the corrosion margin becomes.

| Weld color after cooling | Reading | Action before release |
|---|---|---|
| Bright to light straw | Good control | Passivation may be enough |
| Gold / brown | Marginal oxidation | Clean and passivate; inspect service risk |
| Blue / purple | Poor purge or excess heat | Pickle or mechanically remove, then passivate |
| Gray / black scale | Unacceptable | Grind back, correct procedure, rework as needed |

For visible decorative work, cleaning also has to restore the surface appearance. For hygienic or chloride service, the deeper issue is corrosion resistance, not cosmetic color.

---

## Grade-Family Notes

Austenitic, ferritic, duplex, and martensitic stainless do not weld the same way.

| Family | Main control | Common risk |
|---|---|---|
| Austenitic 304/316 | Heat input, filler carbon, heat tint removal | Sensitization, distortion, hot cracking |
| Ferritic 430/409 | HAZ grain growth and toughness | Brittle weld zone on thicker sections |
| Duplex 2205/2507 | Interpass temperature and ferrite balance | Loss of phase balance, sigma phase |
| Martensitic 410/420 | Preheat and post-weld heat treatment | Hard brittle HAZ cracking |

If the drawing only says "stainless" but the shop has not named the grade family, the WPS is not ready.

## Welding Release Checklist

Before production starts, confirm these items in writing:

- Base metal grade and heat identification.
- Filler designation and filler heat/lot traceability.
- Welding process and WPS number.
- Shielding gas and back-purge requirement.
- Interpass limit and recording method.
- Heat tint removal method: passivation, pickling, grinding, or polish route.
- Inspection route: visual, ferrite check, pressure test, dye penetrant, or project-specific NDT.

This is not paperwork for its own sake. It is how the buyer avoids a weld that looks acceptable at dispatch and becomes the first corrosion site in service.

## Three Field Failures That Keep Repeating

The failures are usually ordinary:

1. The filler spool did not match the base metal, but nobody caught it before welding.
2. Duplex pipework was welded without real interpass control.
3. Blue heat tint was "passivated" but never actually removed.

All three can pass a casual visual check. None of them should pass a serious stainless release.

## FAQ

**Why use L-grade filler on L-grade base metal?** Low-carbon filler helps keep the weld metal and adjacent corrosion performance aligned with the low-carbon base route. For welded corrosion service, it is the safer default.

**Can stainless be welded to carbon steel?** Yes, normally with ER309L/E309L-type transition filler. The filler must handle dilution from the carbon-steel side.

**Is passivation enough to fix heat tint?** Only for very light tint. Blue, purple, gray, or black oxide needs pickling or mechanical removal before passivation makes sense.

**Why is duplex welding stricter?** Duplex performance depends on ferrite-austenite balance. Poor heat control can damage that balance even if the bead looks acceptable.

**Should every stainless weld be back purged?** Not every open plate weld. Tube, pipe, sanitary work, and closed sections usually need it because the root side is part of the service surface.

Stainless welding is manageable when the route is built around the alloy. Before approving the WPS, confirm grade, filler, heat input, purge, and post-weld cleaning. Those five checks prevent most avoidable failures.
