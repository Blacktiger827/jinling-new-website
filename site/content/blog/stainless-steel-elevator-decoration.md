# Which Stainless Finish Still Looks Good Inside an Elevator?

An elevator cab is a harsh little room. People stand close to the panels, lighting is directional, luggage hits the lower wall, cleaners wipe the same hand zones every day, and a tiny mismatch between replacement panels is easy to see.

That is why elevator stainless should not be chosen from a sample board alone. The finish has to match the touch map.

## Start With the Zone

| Cab zone | Safer finish route | Why it works |
|---|---|---|
| Door skins and side walls | Hairline or No.4 | Directional grain hides daily scratches and wipe marks |
| Kick plates and lower panels | No.4, thicker gauge | Scuffs blend better into the grain |
| Ceiling and remote feature panels | Mirror, PVD, or BA | Lower touch, more freedom for visual drama |
| Button-adjacent panels | No.4 + AFP or mirror + AFP | Fingerprints become the maintenance problem |
| Coastal or hospital elevators | 316L route review | Cleaning chemistry and chloride matter more than sample appearance |

Mirror can look stunning at handover. Six months later, the same mirror can look busy with fingerprints, trolley marks, cleaner streaks, and one replacement panel that does not quite match. Hairline may feel quieter on day one, but it often ages better in the parts people touch.

## Hairline Is Usually the Working Luxury Finish

[Hairline](/surfaces/stainless-steel-hairline-finish) reads refined because the grain runs continuously across the sheet. In an elevator, that long vertical line makes the cab feel taller and calmer. It also hides small operational scratches better than mirror because it diffuses light instead of reflecting everything back at one angle.

[No.4](/surfaces/stainless-steel-no4-brushed-finish) is a little more practical and a little less elegant. It is easier to blend after site handling, which makes it useful on door skins, kick plates, service elevators, and busy commercial buildings.

Mirror belongs where the panel is mostly viewed, not handled: ceilings, feature strips, low-touch rear walls, and controlled hospitality interiors. If mirror sits beside hands, luggage, or cleaning spray, the grade, AFP route, film, and maintenance plan all have to be part of the spec.

## When Do PVD and AFP Make Sense?

PVD color works when the design needs gold, bronze, black, champagne, or rose-gold tone. The base finish still matters because PVD does not hide it. Hairline gold reads soft and directional. Mirror black reads dramatic and unforgiving.

AFP is different. It is a clear top route for fingerprints and wipe marks. It does not make a panel scratch-proof, and it does not fix the wrong grade. It earns its cost on high-touch mirror, PVD, and appliance-style elevator panels where the owner wants the surface to stay clean between wiping cycles.

## Grade Still Matters

304 is the default for most dry indoor elevator interiors. 201 can be considered only for genuinely dry, budget interior panels where shorter replacement life is accepted. It should not be used as a quiet substitute in coastal buildings, hospitals, food-service environments, or any cab cleaned with aggressive chemicals.

316L belongs in the conversation when marine air, humid entrances, pool adjacency, or chloride cleaning is part of the real service life. In those environments, the alloy is part of the appearance spec because early staining is highly visible on decorative panels.

## What to Put on the PO

Write the cab by zone, not as one generic finish:

- door skins: 304 hairline, vertical grain, protective film retained until installation
- kick plates: 304 No.4, heavier gauge, grain direction stated
- ceiling feature: 304 mirror or PVD, low-touch location
- coastal cab: 316L review for visible panels, cleaning chemistry confirmed
- high-touch mirror or PVD: AFP top route and approved reference sample

That level of wording prevents the common mistake: choosing one beautiful sample and spreading it across every surface in the cab.
